Arrowhead Plant Care: The Fast-Growing Trailing Plant That Forgives Beginners

The arrowhead plant, or syngonium, is one of the easiest tropical houseplants to keep in a flat — and one of the fastest to outgrow its spot. It starts as a neat plant with arrow-shaped leaves, then sends out long stems, and if you give it something to climb, its leaves grow larger and develop extra lobes — as in the wild, where it climbs trees in Central and South American rainforests.

Short version: bright indirect light, water when the top few centimetres feel dry, and do not fuss over humidity the way you would for a calathea. Prune long stems back when it gets leggy, and root the cuttings in water — they take a couple of weeks. It usually survives a beginner's learning curve.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • Bright indirect light: near an east window, or a short distance back from a brighter one. Variegated types lose their white or pink colouring in dim rooms.
  • Water when the top dries out: check the top few centimetres with a finger.
  • Average indoor humidity is usually enough: it does not crisp like a calathea.
  • Prune to keep it bushy: cut long stems back to just above a leaf node. Cuttings root easily in water.
  • Trail or climb — pick one: let it spill from a shelf, or give it a moss pole to climb.
  • Keep it away from pets that chew: syngonium leaves and stems are toxic if eaten.

Where to Put It

A syngonium wants bright indirect light: an east-facing window with gentle morning sun, a north-facing window with nothing blocking it, or a spot back from a south or west window, ideally with a sheer curtain. It tolerates a dimmer spot better than most tropical plants, but growth gets leggy, new leaves come out smaller, and variegated varieties lose their white, cream, or pink markings. If the variegation is fading, check the light first, not food or water.

Then decide what you want it to be. Let it trail from a shelf, a hanging basket, or the top of a bookcase — it looks best when the stems spill, and it needs the least attention. Our guide to hanging plants for small apartments includes trailing plants that suit exactly this treatment. Or give it something to climb — a moss pole or trellis — and tie the stems loosely to it as they grow; the leaves get larger and develop the lobed mature shape. Or keep it compact by pruning regularly, for a bushy tabletop plant.

Watering, Step by Step

A syngonium wants its soil moist but not wet, and it is forgiving about the timing.

  1. Check with a finger, not a calendar. If the top few centimetres feel dry, it is time; if they still feel moist, wait. The finger test in our guide to how often to water indoor plants is the same habit, applied to a plant that tolerates a wider margin of error than most.
  2. Water thoroughly, pouring around the whole surface until a little runs from the drainage holes.
  3. Empty the saucer afterwards. A syngonium likes moist soil, not a pot standing in water — roots left sitting in water lead to the problems in our root rot guide.
  4. Use room-temperature water, and water less often in winter, when growth slows and the plant drinks less.

A syngonium that has dried out completely will usually droop and recover within a day of watering, while a plant that stays wet yellows from the base and does not bounce back.

Soil, Pots, and Feeding

A syngonium wants a soil mix that holds moisture without staying heavy: ordinary houseplant compost with some bark, perlite, or coir. The pot must have drainage holes — grow it in a plain pot with holes and slip that inside the decorative one. Repot in spring when roots crowd the surface, moving up just one pot size — see our guide to repotting a houseplant in an apartment. Feed lightly in the brighter months only.

Pruning and Propagation

Left alone, a syngonium eventually becomes a long bare stem with leaves only at the end. Pruning prevents that, and it is also how you get free plants. Cut just above a leaf node — the small bump where a leaf joins the stem, often with a tiny aerial root nearby. New growth branches from below the cut, so the plant fills out. A leggy syngonium can be cut back by half in spring and will usually branch from several nodes at once. Pinch the growing tips of young plants for a bushy tabletop plant — a pinched tip branches into two.

Syngonium cuttings are among the easiest to root, and the routine is the same one in our guide to propagating pothos in water. Cut a stem just below a node, keeping one or two leaves — a cutting without a node will not root. Put it in a jar of water with the node submerged and the leaves above the water line. Roots usually appear within a couple of weeks. Once they are a few centimetres long, pot the cutting into the same open mix as the parent, in bright indirect light.

Reading the Plant

Long bare stems with small leaves far apart mean not enough light, or a plant that has never been pruned — move it closer to the window and cut the bare stems back in spring. Variegation fading to plain green means light too dim for the variety; move it brighter and judge it by the new leaves. Yellow leaves, mostly the oldest ones, often mean overwatering; a single old leaf yellowing is normal. Brown, crispy tips point to dry air, underwatering, or salts. Leaves drooping all at once usually mean thirst; a syngonium usually recovers within a day of watering. Fine webbing under the leaves is possibly spider mites — see our spider mite guide.

Common Mistakes

  • Never pruning it. Left alone it becomes a bare vine; a spring cut-back keeps it bushy.
  • Keeping a variegated type in a dim corner. It survives, but it turns green. Light keeps the colour.
  • Watering by the calendar. Its thirst changes with the weather and the heating.
  • Letting the pot stand in water. The fastest route to yellow leaves.
  • Throwing cuttings away. Every pruned stem with a node is a free plant — root them in water.

Quick Questions, Straight Answers

How often should I water an arrowhead plant?

When the top few centimetres of soil feel dry — check with a finger every few days rather than following a fixed schedule. Water thoroughly, then empty the saucer.

Why is my syngonium getting leggy?

Usually not enough light, a plant that has never been pruned, or both. Move it brighter and cut the long bare stems back to just above a leaf node in spring.

Why is my variegated syngonium turning green?

Low light is the usual cause. Variegated varieties need bright indirect light to keep their white, cream, or pink markings; in a dim room the plant produces plain green leaves. Move it brighter.

Can I propagate an arrowhead plant from a cutting?

Yes, easily. Cut a stem just below a node, with a leaf or two, and stand it in a jar of water with the node submerged. Roots usually appear within a couple of weeks, and then pot the cutting into ordinary houseplant mix.
