Arrowhead Plant Care: The Fast-Growing Trailing Plant That Forgives Beginners

The arrowhead plant, or syngonium, is one of the easiest tropical houseplants to keep in a flat — and one of the fastest to outgrow its spot. It starts as a neat little plant with arrow-shaped leaves, then sends out long trailing stems, and if you give it something to climb, its leaves gradually change shape, growing larger and developing extra lobes. That is not a problem to fix; it is the plant doing what it does in the wild, where it climbs trees in the rainforests of Central and South America.

Here is the short version: give it bright indirect light, water when the top few centimetres of soil feel dry, and do not fuss over humidity the way you would for a calathea. Prune the long stems back when it gets leggy, and use the cuttings to grow new plants — they root in water in a couple of weeks. Results vary by light and variety, but a syngonium is one of the few tropical plants that usually survives a beginner's learning curve and still looks good.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • Bright indirect light is the sweet spot: near an east window, or a short distance back from a brighter one. It tolerates lower light than most tropical plants, but variegated types lose their white or pink colouring in dim rooms.
  • Water when the top dries out: check the top few centimetres with a finger and water thoroughly when they feel dry. It forgives a missed watering better than most tropical plants.
  • Average indoor humidity is usually enough: it appreciates a bit of extra moisture, but it does not crisp the way calatheas and ferns do.
  • Prune to keep it bushy: cut long stems back to just above a leaf node and the plant branches from below the cut. The cuttings root easily in water.
  • Trail or climb — pick one: let it spill from a shelf or hanging basket, or give it a moss pole or trellis to climb and watch the leaves grow larger and change shape.
  • Keep it away from pets that chew: like most aroids, syngonium leaves and stems are toxic if eaten.

Where to Put It: Light First, Then the Trail-or-Climb Choice

A syngonium wants bright indirect light: an east-facing window with gentle morning sun, a north-facing window with nothing blocking it, or a spot a short distance back from a south or west window, ideally with a sheer curtain between the plant and strong direct sun. It tolerates a dimmer spot better than most tropical plants — it will keep growing, slowly, in light that would stall a calathea — but growth gets leggy, new leaves come out smaller, and variegated varieties gradually lose their white, cream, or pink markings and turn plain green. If the variegation is fading, light is the first thing to check, not food or water.

Then decide what you want the plant to be. Let it trail from a shelf, a hanging basket, or the top of a bookcase — it looks best when the stems are allowed to spill, and it is the lowest-maintenance option. Our guide to hanging plants for small apartments includes trailing plants that suit exactly this treatment. Or give it something to climb — a moss pole, a trellis, or a coir pole — and tie the stems loosely to it as they grow. The leaves get larger and start to develop the lobed mature shape. Or keep it compact by pruning regularly, which is the option if you want a bushy tabletop plant.

Turn the pot a quarter-turn every week or so so all sides get the light. In winter the light drops, so a syngonium that was happy a metre from the window in summer may want to move closer — our guide to winter-proofing your indoor plants covers the heating-season routine for a whole collection.

Watering, Step by Step: The Forgiving Middle

A syngonium wants its soil moist but not wet, and it is more forgiving about the timing than most tropical plants.

  1. Check with a finger, not a calendar. Press a fingertip into the surface. If the top few centimetres feel dry, it is time; if they still feel moist, wait. The finger test in our guide to how often to water indoor plants is the same habit, applied here to a plant that tolerates a wider margin of error than most.
  2. Water thoroughly and slowly, pouring around the whole surface until a little runs from the drainage holes.
  3. Empty the saucer or cachepot afterwards. A syngonium likes moist soil, not a pot standing in water — roots left sitting in water are the road to the problems in our root rot guide.
  4. Use room-temperature water where you can, and water less often in winter, when growth slows and the plant drinks less. The check, not the calendar, is what changes.

A syngonium that has been allowed to dry out completely will usually droop dramatically and then recover within a day of a thorough watering — it is one of the plants that tells you clearly when it is thirsty and forgives you for it. A plant that stays wet, by contrast, yellows from the base and does not bounce back, which is why the drainage holes and the empty saucer matter more than the exact number of days between waterings.

Soil, Pots, and Feeding: Light Mix, Light Touch

A syngonium wants a soil mix that holds moisture without staying heavy and airless: ordinary houseplant compost with some added bark, perlite, or coir to keep it open works well. The pot must have drainage holes — grow it in a plain pot with holes and slip that inside the decorative one if you prefer the look, and empty any water that collects in the outer pot.

Repot in spring when roots crowd the surface or water runs through very fast, moving up just one pot size. The method in our guide to repotting a houseplant in an apartment was written for exactly this kitchen-table situation. A syngonium that has outgrown its pot is also a good candidate for a moss pole at the same time, since you can set the pole in place while the root ball is out.

Feeding is a light touch. During the brighter months, when the plant is actively growing, a diluted balanced houseplant feed given occasionally is plenty. In autumn and winter, when growth slows, stop feeding altogether. Over-feeding shows up as brown leaf tips and salts building up on the soil surface; if that happens, flush the pot once with plenty of plain water, let it drain freely, and skip the next feeds. Average indoor humidity is usually enough for a syngonium, and brown tips from dry air are uncommon unless the plant sits near a radiator — grouping plants or a pebble tray is a bonus, not a requirement.

Pruning and Propagation: Cut It Back, Grow New Plants

Left alone, a syngonium eventually becomes a long bare stem with leaves only at the end. Pruning is how you prevent that, and it is also how you get free plants. Cut just above a leaf node — the small bump on the stem where a leaf joins it, often with a tiny aerial root nearby. New growth branches from just below the cut, so the plant fills out instead of stretching. Cut back as hard as you like in spring: a leggy syngonium can be cut back by half or more and will usually branch from several nodes at once. Pinch the growing tips of young plants through the growing season if you want a bushy tabletop plant — a pinched tip branches into two, and repeated pinching builds a full plant.

Syngonium cuttings are among the easiest houseplant cuttings to root, and the routine is the same one in our guide to propagating pothos in water, which works because both plants are aroids with the same node-rooting habit. Cut a stem just below a node, keeping one or two leaves on the cutting — a cutting without a node will not root. Put it in a jar of water with the node submerged and the leaves above the water line, and change the water every few days. In warm, bright conditions, roots usually appear within a couple of weeks. Once they are a few centimetres long, pot the cutting into the same open mix as the parent and keep it in bright indirect light while it settles. Several cuttings in one pot make a full plant faster than a single stem.

Reading the Plant: Leggy, Pale, and Brown Tips

Long bare stems with small leaves far apart mean not enough light, or a plant that has never been pruned — move it closer to the window and cut the bare stems back in spring. Variegation fading to plain green usually means light too dim for the variety; move the plant to a brighter spot, and know that a leaf that has already turned green will not regain its white markings — judge it by the new leaves. Yellow leaves, mostly the oldest ones, often mean overwatering, especially if the soil stays wet; check the drainage and let the soil dry more between waterings. A single old leaf yellowing while the plant grows is normal. Brown, crispy leaf tips point to dry air, underwatering, or salts from over-feeding — work through them in that order. Leaves drooping all at once usually mean thirst, and a syngonium usually recovers within a day of a thorough watering; if it does not recover, check the roots. Fine webbing or tiny speckled damage under the leaves is possibly spider mites — the shower-and-wipe routine in our spider mite guide is the practical first response.

Common Mistakes

  • Never pruning it. A syngonium left alone becomes a bare vine. A spring cut-back is what keeps it a bushy plant.
  • Keeping a variegated type in a dim corner. It will survive, but it will turn green and stretch. Light is what keeps the colour.
  • Watering by the calendar. Its thirst changes with the weather, the heating, and the size of the pot.
  • Letting the pot stand in water. The fastest route to yellow leaves and root problems.
  • Throwing cuttings away. Every pruned stem with a node is a free plant. Root them in water and pot them up.
  • Expecting the leaves to stay arrow-shaped forever. Mature climbing leaves grow larger and develop extra lobes. That is the plant growing up, not a problem.

Quick Questions, Straight Answers

How often should I water an arrowhead plant?

When the top few centimetres of soil feel dry — check with a finger every few days rather than following a fixed schedule. That may be about once a week in a warm bright spot and less in a cool one. Water thoroughly until a little runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer. Results vary by light, temperature, and pot size.

Why is my syngonium getting leggy?

Usually not enough light, a plant that has never been pruned, or both. Move it to a brighter spot and cut the long bare stems back to just above a leaf node in spring — it will branch from below the cut. Root the cuttings in water and you get new plants from the same job.

Why is my variegated syngonium turning green?

Low light is the usual cause. Variegated varieties need bright indirect light to keep their white, cream, or pink markings; in a dim room the plant produces plain green leaves, which photosynthesise more efficiently. Move it brighter and judge it by the new leaves — old green leaves will not regain their markings.

Can I propagate an arrowhead plant from a cutting?

Yes, easily. Cut a stem just below a node, keeping one or two leaves, and stand it in a jar of water with the node submerged. Roots usually appear within a couple of weeks. Once they are a few centimetres long, pot the cutting into ordinary houseplant mix and keep it in bright indirect light while it settles.
