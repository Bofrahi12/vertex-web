How to Propagate Pothos in Water: Cut at the Node, Root It, Pot It Up

A pothos vine is a plant that wants to make more plants. Along every stem are small bumps — nodes — that are ready to grow roots the moment they sit in water. That means one leggy vine from a friend, a shop, or your own shelf can become several new plants for the cost of a clean jar and a pair of scissors. No greenhouse, no special equipment, and in an apartment the whole operation fits on one windowsill.

This guide walks the full journey, including the part most quick tutorials skip: cuttings are rarely lost while they root in water — they are lost in the move from water to soil. Get the node right, keep the water routine simple, and handle that transition gently, and pothos propagation is one of the most reliable projects a beginner can try. Not every cutting makes it, and results vary with light, temperature, and the health of the mother vine — but the odds are firmly on your side.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • Cut: just below a node (the bump where a leaf joins the stem), keeping one or two leaves on the cutting.
  • Submerge: the node goes under water; the leaves stay above it. Strip any leaf that would sit in the water.
  • Light: bright, indirect light on a sill or shelf — never harsh direct sun on the jar.
  • Water: top up as needed and refresh it about weekly so it stays clear and oxygenated.
  • Pot up: when roots are several centimetres long and branching, move to soil — then keep that soil consistently moist for the first couple of weeks.

First, Find the Node (This Is the Whole Trick)

Run a pothos stem between your fingers. At each point where a leaf stalk meets the stem there is a slight thickening — the node. You may also see a small brownish knob nearby: an aerial root waiting for something to climb or root into.

Roots grow from nodes, not from random stem and not from a leaf on its own. A cutting without a node in the water can sit in a jar for weeks looking green and doing nothing. A cutting with its node submerged is the entire method — everything else is care and patience.

A good cutting is roughly 10–15 cm (4–6 inches) of healthy stem with one node near its lower end and one or two leaves at the top. Take cuttings from green, firm growth; avoid yellowed, damaged, or very woody sections. If your mother plant's leaves are yellowing for other reasons, sort that first — our pothos yellow leaves guide covers the common causes.

Step by Step: From Vine to Jar

  1. Choose your vine. A long, trailing stem you were going to trim anyway is ideal — pruning the mother plant and propagating are the same afternoon's work. (Bonus: cutting a vine back often encourages the plant to branch.)
  2. Cut below the node. With clean scissors or a sharp knife, cut about a centimetre below a node. Clean tools matter: wash them first so you are not introducing rot into an open cut.
  3. Trim the cutting. Remove the leaf nearest the cut end if it would sit underwater — leaves submerged in water rot, and rot spreads to the stem. Keep one or two leaves at the top; they power root growth.
  4. Set it in water. Use a clean glass jar, bottle, or vase with fresh tap water at room temperature. The node must be fully submerged; the leaves must be fully out. A narrow-necked bottle holds stems upright in small spaces.
  5. Place it in bright, indirect light. A windowsill that does not bake in afternoon sun, or a shelf near a bright window, works well in most flats.
  6. Refresh the water about weekly. Tip out most of the old water, rinse the jar if it feels slimy, refill. Top up between changes so the node never dries out — a node left high and dry mid-rooting often stalls.
  7. Wait, and watch the node. First root nubs commonly appear within a couple of weeks in warm, bright conditions, and more slowly in cool or dim spots. White, firm roots are healthy; brown, mushy stem ends are not (see Troubleshooting).

The Apartment Propagation Station

You do not need a product with "propagation station" in its name — but arranging your jars in one place makes the routine effortless and turns cuttings into decor while they root:

  • A windowsill row: small jars and bottles lined along the sill — the classic, and it costs nothing but saved jam jars.
  • A single shelf near the window: use a tray under the jars to catch drips and protect the shelf when you change water.
  • A spare corner of the kitchen: often the brightest room, and the sink is right there for weekly water changes.
  • Hang it: a cutting in a small bottle can hang in a macramé holder by the window if sill space is gone — see our hanging plants guide for small-space display ideas.

Keep the station somewhere you look every day. The weekly water change only happens if the jars are somewhere you will notice them clouding.

When and How to Move Cuttings to Soil

Wait until the roots are several centimetres long and starting to branch — a single short root is fragile and easy to snap, and cuttings moved too early struggle. Then:

  1. Prepare a small pot with a drainage hole and fresh, general houseplant mix, lightly moistened.
  2. Make a hole with a pencil or chopstick, lower the roots in gently, and firm the mix around the stem so the plant stands at a natural depth.
  3. Water it in until a little drains from the bottom, and empty the saucer after it has drained.
  4. Keep the soil evenly moist for the first couple of weeks. This is the step that decides success. Water roots are adapted to constant moisture; dropped straight into soil that dries out between waterings, they often fail. Moist (not soggy) soil at first, then gradually settle into a normal pothos rhythm as new leaf growth appears.
  5. Bright, indirect light, no fertilizer yet. Wait until you see new growth before feeding; fresh roots in new soil do not need it.

Planting several cuttings together in one pot gives a fuller plant much faster than a single stem — a good use for a whole trimmed vine. Our apartment repotting guide covers mess-free potting technique if you are working at a kitchen table.

Or Keep It in Water Long-Term

Pothos can also simply live in water indefinitely, trailing from a bottle on a shelf. If you go this route: change the water regularly (it is now the plant's only environment), rinse algae from the glass, and understand the trade-off — growth in plain water is usually slower and leaner than in soil, because water carries no nutrients. A drop of diluted houseplant fertilizer in the water now and then is a common approach, used sparingly. Many people enjoy water jars as the display and soil pots as the "real" plants; both are legitimate.

Troubleshooting

  • Stem end turning brown and mushy: rot. Cut the stem back above the damaged part to clean, firm tissue (making sure a node remains), rinse, and restart in fresh water in a washed jar.
  • No roots after several weeks: check the basics — is a node actually underwater? Is the jar in near-darkness? Is the room cold? Fix the most obvious one and give it more time before giving up; slow is not the same as failed.
  • Cloudy water or green algae: change the water, wash the jar, and move it out of direct sun. A jar in bright indirect light grows roots without growing an algae farm.
  • Leaves yellowing in the jar: often an old leaf ageing, or a leaf that sat in the water. Remove it, refresh the water, and check the stem is still firm.
  • Cutting wilts right after potting: usually the transition dryness problem. Water thoroughly, keep the soil moist, and give it a week — many cuttings recover as the roots adjust.

Common Mistakes

  • Cutting without a node. The single most common failure, and it is a choosing error, not bad luck. Always locate the bump first.
  • Leaves underwater. They rot and take the cutting with them. Strip the lower leaf.
  • Forgetting the water change. Stagnant, slimy water invites the rot you are trying to avoid.
  • Potting up too early. One tiny root is not a root system. Wait for length and branching.
  • Treating a new soil cutting like an established plant. The first fortnight's consistent moisture is the whole game — set a reminder if you need one.

FAQ

How long does pothos take to root in water?

It varies with light, warmth, and the cutting. In bright, warm conditions, root nubs often show within one to two weeks and a plantable root system a few weeks after that. In a cool, dim spot it can take considerably longer. There is no fixed date to judge by — judge by the roots themselves.

Can I propagate a single leaf?

No. A leaf alone has no node, and roots (and new stems) grow from nodes. You need a stem section that includes at least one node.

Should I use rooting hormone?

You can, but pothos is one of the plants that least needs it — nodes root readily in plain water. Save the hormone for woodier, more reluctant plants.

Why did my cutting root beautifully, then die in soil?

Almost always the transition: water-grown roots moved into soil that dried out. Next time, pot up when roots are a bit longer and keep the soil consistently moist for the first couple of weeks while the roots adapt.

Can I put the new plant back with the mother plant?

Yes — planting rooted cuttings into the top of the mother plant's pot is a popular way to fill out a sparse, leggy pothos. Use the same moist-first-fortnight routine.
