Root Rot in Houseplants: How to Spot It Early and Save the Plant

A wilting plant usually makes you reach for the watering can. With root rot, that instinct is exactly the problem: the plant looks thirsty, the soil is already wet, and more water pushes it further in the wrong direction. Root rot is what happens when roots sit in wet, airless soil for too long and start to decay — and a decayed root cannot drink, which is why the leaves above it droop as if the pot were bone dry.

The good news is that the diagnosis takes about two minutes and costs nothing: you look at the roots. This guide walks through the early signs, the one check that settles the question, a rescue you can do at a kitchen sink or in a bathtub in a small flat, and — honestly — the point at which saving the roots stops being realistic and taking cuttings is the smarter move. Results vary by plant, by how much healthy root is left, and by light and temperature while it recovers, but a plant caught early often comes back well.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • The classic pattern: leaves wilting or yellowing while the soil is still wet, days after watering. Thirst and rot can look identical above the soil — they are opposites below it.
  • The deciding check: slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots are firm and pale; rotted roots are brown or black, soft or slimy, and may smell sour or musty.
  • The rescue, in one line: unpot, rinse, cut away every soft dark root with clean scissors, repot into fresh free-draining mix in a pot with a drainage hole, then water lightly.
  • Do not water a suspect plant "to help it". Until you have seen the roots, extra water is the one thing that can only make rot worse.
  • The usual apartment cause: a decorative pot with no drainage hole, or a nursery pot left sitting in water inside a cover pot.
  • If no firm pale roots remain, the plant itself is unlikely to recover — take healthy stem cuttings instead, while there is still something healthy to take.

The Early Signs: What Root Rot Looks Like Above the Soil

No single leaf symptom proves root rot, because wilting and yellowing have several possible causes. What should raise your suspicion is the combination — and especially the timing against watering.

  • Wilting that watering does not fix. A thirsty plant usually perks up within hours of a good drink. A plant that stays limp in wet soil is telling you the roots are not working — see also our watering guide for how long soil should normally take to dry.
  • Yellowing lower leaves, spreading upward. Old leaves go first, then the problem climbs. Pothos owners often meet this pattern first as yellow leaves — our pothos yellow leaves guide helps you separate rot from the other common causes.
  • Soil that stays wet for a long time. If the surface is still damp well over a week after watering in a normally warm room, the plant is not drinking at its usual rate, or the mix holds too much water, or both.
  • A sour or musty smell at the pot, or tiny fungus gnats hovering at the soil surface. Gnats alone do not mean rot — they like any damp mix — but gnats plus a wilting plant plus wet soil is a pattern worth acting on; our fungus gnat guide covers clearing them once the soil problem is fixed.
  • Stems soft or dark at the base, or a plant that feels loose in its pot, as if it is no longer anchored. This is a later sign — check the roots the same day.

If the soil is dry and the plant is limp, that is ordinary thirst: water it. If the soil is wet and the plant is limp, do not guess. Check.

The Two-Minute Root Check

This is the step most guides mention and many readers skip, because unpotting a plant feels drastic. It is not — done gently, a healthy plant will not mind it at all, and it is the only way to know.

  1. Work somewhere washable. In a flat, that usually means inside the bathtub, in a large sink, or on an old towel over the kitchen counter. Lay everything out before you start: clean scissors, fresh mix, a pot with a drainage hole, and a bag for the old soil.
  2. Slide the root ball out. Tip the pot on its side, support the stems with one hand, and ease the ball out with the other. Squeeze a flexible plastic nursery pot gently if it sticks. Avoid dragging the plant out by its stems.
  3. Read the roots. Firm, pale roots — white, cream, or light tan depending on the plant — are alive and working. Brown or black roots that feel soft, stringy, or slimy between your fingers are rotted. A healthy root ball smells like soil; a rotted one often smells sour.
  4. Judge how much is left. A few dark roots among many firm pale ones is an early case. Half the root system soft is a serious case that can still often be saved. No firm roots at all is the cuttings situation described below.

The Rescue: Step by Step at the Sink

Work calmly and finish the job in one session — a plant sitting bare-root on a counter dries out while you hunt for supplies.

  1. Remove the old soil. Gently crumble and shake the old mix away from the roots over the bath or a bag. Rinse the roots under a gentle stream of lukewarm tap water so you can actually see what you are cutting. Old, wet, sour-smelling mix goes in the bin, not back in a pot — do not reuse it for another plant.
  2. Cut away every rotted root. With clean scissors, trim each soft, dark, or slimy root back to firm, pale tissue. Be thorough rather than hopeful: a piece of rot left behind can keep spreading, while a plant can regrow roots it has lost. Wipe or rinse the scissors if they get coated, especially before any final tidy cuts.
  3. Trim the top to match. If you removed a large share of the roots, remove a similar share of the weakest, most yellowed growth above ground. Fewer roots cannot support the same canopy, and the plant recovers better in balance. Keep the healthiest stems and leaves.
  4. Repot into fresh, free-draining mix. Use a clean pot with a drainage hole — the same size as before, or one size smaller if the root system is now much reduced; a big pot of wet mix around a small root ball recreates the original problem. A standard houseplant mix lightened with perlite or fine bark drains well for most common houseplants.
  5. Water once, lightly. Settle the mix around the roots with a light watering, let the pot drain fully in the sink, and empty any saucer. Then leave it alone: the next watering waits until the top of the mix has dried out.
  6. Give it recovery conditions. Bright indirect light, a warm room away from radiators and cold draughts, and no fertiliser until you see new growth. Recovery is measured in weeks, not days: expect existing damaged leaves to stay damaged, and judge success by new leaves and roots, not by the old foliage turning back.

That sink-side routine is deliberately the same, tidy approach as our guide to repotting a houseplant in an apartment — the bathtub method keeps soil and rinse water off a renter's floors.

When the Honest Answer Is Cuttings, Not Rescue

If every root is soft, or the stem itself is mushy well above the soil line, repotting rarely saves that plant, and spending weeks on it can mean losing the healthy top growth too. Many common apartment plants give you a second route while there is still firm stem left:

  • Pothos, philodendron, and monstera: cut healthy stem sections that include a node and root them in water or fresh mix — the node-first method in our pothos propagation guide applies to most trailing aroids.
  • Snake plants and ZZ plants: these store reserves in thick leaves and rhizomes and rot from the base up. Save any firm, healthy rhizome sections or leaves, let cut surfaces dry before replanting, and keep the new mix barely moist while they re-root — their normal care in our snake plant guide and ZZ plant guide is deliberately on the dry side for this reason.
  • If the stem is soft all the way up and no firm, healthy section remains, let the plant go. That is not a failure of effort — rot found that late has usually been developing unseen for a long time, and the useful lesson is in the prevention list below.

Why It Happens in Apartments (and How to Stop a Repeat)

Rot is rarely caused by one huge overwatering. It is usually a flat-specific setup quietly stacking the odds:

  • The cover-pot puddle. The plant lives in a nursery pot inside a decorative pot with no hole. Water drains into the cover pot and the plant sits in it for days. Fix: after watering, drain in the sink and empty the cover pot every time — or check it ten minutes later and tip out what collected.
  • A pot with no drainage hole at all. Without an exit, judging how much water is in the bottom is guesswork. Fix: keep plants in nursery pots with holes and treat the pretty pot as a sleeve.
  • Mix that stays wet too long. Dense, old, compacted soil holds water and excludes air. Fix: refresh the mix when you repot, and choose a free-draining blend rather than the heaviest bag on the shelf.
  • Watering by calendar. A plant by a bright window and the same plant in a dim hallway dry out at completely different speeds, and both slow down in winter. Fix: check the soil first — the finger test or lifting the pot to feel its weight — and let the plant, not the date, decide.
  • Going up too many pot sizes. A small root system in a large volume of mix cannot use the moisture held at the edges, so the mix stays wet. Fix: one size up at a time.
  • Low light slowing everything down. A plant that is barely photosynthesising is barely drinking. If a plant must live in a dim corner, it usually needs noticeably less frequent watering than the same plant in bright light — another reason the low-light picks in our low-light plants guide are mostly plants that tolerate dry spells well.

Apartment Examples

The monstera in the pretty pot

A monstera in a ceramic cover pot is watered weekly. It starts wilting between waterings, so it gets watered more. Tipped out, the nursery pot has been standing in a centimetre of water inside the cover pot, and the lowest roots are dark and soft, while the upper roots are still firm and pale. Rescue: rinse, trim the dark roots, fresh mix, drain fully after every watering and empty the cover pot. Recovery sign to watch for: a new leaf unfurling normally over the following weeks.

The small fern in the small pot — the opposite trap

A small fern wilts, but its soil is dry, not wet, and its roots are pale and firm: this is thirst, not rot, and a thorough soak revives it. The lesson of keeping both possibilities in mind is the whole article in miniature — same droop, opposite fixes, and the root check is what tells them apart in two minutes.

Common Mistakes

  • Watering a wilting plant without checking the soil first. With rot, that "rescue" water is the thing doing the damage.
  • Cutting rot back timidly. Leaving soft dark roots "because the plant needs roots" leaves the decay in place. Firm roots regrow; rotted roots do not recover.
  • Repotting into the same old soil. The old mix is wet, compacted, and sour. Fresh mix is a core part of the treatment, not an optional extra.
  • Putting the rescued plant back into the same no-drainage setup that caused the problem — including the puddle in the cover pot.
  • Feeding a recovering plant to "give it strength". Fertiliser on damaged roots and a plant that is not yet growing again adds stress. Wait for new growth.
  • Judging by the old leaves. Yellowed and damaged foliage does not turn back. Judge a rescue by new growth over the next few weeks, and remove leaves as they finish declining.

FAQ

Can a plant recover from root rot?

Often, yes — if some firm, healthy roots remain and the rotted parts are cut away promptly, then most common houseplants can regrow a working root system over the following weeks. Recovery is much less likely when no healthy root or firm stem remains, which is why the early root check matters more than any product.

What does root rot smell like?

Rotted roots and the soil around them often smell sour, musty, or like stagnant water, rather than the plain earthy smell of a healthy root ball. Smell is a supporting clue, not a diagnosis on its own — the look and feel of the roots (dark, soft, slimy versus pale and firm) is the deciding test.

Should I cut off all the brown roots?

Cut away every root that is both dark and soft or slimy, back to firm tissue. Some healthy roots are naturally tan or brownish on certain plants, so use texture as the judge: firm is kept, mushy goes, however it is coloured.

Do I need a fungicide or hydrogen peroxide?

Most home rescues do not need either. Removing the rotted tissue, replacing the wet mix, restoring drainage, and correcting the watering routine addresses the conditions that allowed the rot. A plant kept in the same wet setup will usually decline again whatever it was treated with.

How long does recovery take?

Expect weeks. A rescued plant may sit quietly while it rebuilds roots before any new leaf appears, and results vary by species, season, and how much root was lost. Keep the light bright and indirect, the watering restrained, and resist re-unpotting it to check — give the new roots time to do their work.

How do I know it was not just overwatering without rot?

If you unpot early and the roots are still firm and pale, you caught it at the overwatering stage: let the mix dry appropriately, fix the drainage, adjust the routine, and no cutting is needed. Rot is what overwatered roots can become when they stay wet and airless too long — which is exactly why checking early is worthwhile.

Root rot feels like a sudden disaster, but it is usually a slow, quiet process that only becomes visible at the leaves at the end. That is also the hopeful part: the moment you start reading wilting-in-wet-soil as a signal to look at the roots, you start catching it while it is still a twenty-minute sink job — a rinse, a firm trim, fresh mix, a pot that drains — instead of a goodbye. Check first, cut cleanly, and give the plant dry-ish, bright, patient weeks to rebuild.
