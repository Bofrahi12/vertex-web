Plants for a Bathroom With No Window: What Actually Works (and What to Rotate)

It is the most common plant question in flats, and the one most often answered dishonestly: "Which plant will live in my bathroom? It has no window." The lists online will happily sell you a fern for a dark room. The fern will not argue. It will just decline slowly, over a few weeks, in a way that makes you think you did something wrong.

So here is the honest starting point, before any plant names: no houseplant thrives indefinitely in a room with no light at all. Light is food. A plant in true darkness is living off stored reserves, and reserves run out. But that is not the end of the story — because a windowless bathroom in a real flat is rarely used in darkness, and there are two systems that genuinely work: rotating plants between the bathroom and a bright room, and adding a small grow light on a timer. This guide covers both, then names the plants that handle the bathroom side of the deal — the humidity, the dim spells, the splashes — best.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • No plant lives forever in zero light. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you a plant, not advising you.
  • System 1 — Rotation: keep two plants, one on display in the bathroom and one recovering in bright light, and swap them every one to two weeks. This works with no equipment at all.
  • System 2 — Grow light: a small LED grow light on a plug-in timer turns a windowless bathroom into a workable plant spot permanently. This is the only "fit and forget" answer.
  • Best rotation candidates: pothos, snake plant, ZZ plant, spider plant, and cast iron plant — all tolerate low-light spells and bathroom humidity well.
  • Avoid as permanent residents without a grow light: ferns, calatheas, and herbs. They are the plants most often recommended for bathrooms, and the ones that fade fastest in the dark.
  • Watch the other bathroom hazards too: cleaning sprays on leaves, cold draughts from an extractor, and soil that never dries in steamy air.

Why Bathrooms Are Half Perfect

A bathroom gives a plant two things it usually loves and one thing it cannot live without.

  • Humidity: a genuine plus. Showers and baths push moisture into the air regularly, and many common houseplants prefer that to dry, heated living-room air. Our winter humidity guide covers how much difference this makes for thin-leaved plants. In a bathroom, much of that work is done for you.
  • Warmth: usually fine. Bathrooms in most flats sit at ordinary room temperature, which suits the common houseplants named here.
  • Light: the missing piece. An interior bathroom with no window may only receive light when the door is open or the ceiling light is on — and ordinary room lighting, used for minutes at a time, is not a substitute for daylight. A plant reads that as deep shade, all day, every day.

That mix — kind air, absent light — is why the rotation works so well: the bathroom is a pleasant short stay, not a permanent home.

System 1: The Two-Plant Rotation (No Equipment)

This is the practical starting point for most flats, and it costs nothing beyond a second plant.

  1. 1. Choose two plants of the rotation-friendly kind (the list below), in pots the same size if you can, so swapping them takes seconds.
  2. 2. Plant A lives in the bathroom — on the sink edge, a shelf, or hanging from a hook if your tenancy allows one.
  3. 3. Plant B lives in your brightest room, on or near the shelf where your other plants already do well.
  4. 4. Swap them every one to two weeks. A week is a comfortable rhythm for most of the plants below; two weeks is the upper end, and the plant should go back to bright light looking much as it arrived. Put the swap on an existing habit — laundry day, the weekly clean — so it actually happens.
  5. 5. Water in the bright room, not the bathroom. Check the soil when the plant comes out of the bathroom; steamy air keeps soil moist longer than you expect, and the most common rotation mistake is watering on schedule instead of on need. Our watering guide covers the simple finger test.

If a plant comes out of its bathroom week looking pale, stretched, or dropping leaves, shorten its stay — a few days at a time — or move it to the grow-light system below. Results vary by plant and by how dark your bathroom really is; the plant's condition on swap day is your measurement.

System 2: A Small Grow Light on a Timer (The Permanent Fix)

If you want a plant that simply lives in the bathroom, full time, a grow light is the honest answer — and in a small room it is a modest setup, not a laboratory.

  • The light: a small LED grow bulb or panel made for houseplants. A bulb that fits an existing fitting or a small lamp you can clamp or stand safely away from splashes keeps the bathroom looking like a bathroom. Our grow light guide for apartments compares the formats.
  • The timer is the important part. A plug-in timer switches the light on for a long, regular stretch each day — including the hours you are at work — and off at night, when plants need darkness too. Consistency is what a window would have given the plant; the timer provides it.
  • Distance matters. Follow the light maker's guidance for how close the plant should sit; too far and the benefit fades, too close and leaves can scorch. Start at the recommended distance and watch new growth — compact, normal-coloured leaves are the sign you have it roughly right.
  • Safety in a wet room comes first. Keep electrical fittings, timers, and cables positioned and rated appropriately for a bathroom, well away from direct splashes and the shower, and if you are unsure about a fitting in a wet zone, ask an electrician rather than improvising. In a rented flat, a battery or USB lamp is not a shortcut around this — use equipment suited to the room.

With a timed light, the plant list widens: the rotation plants below can stay permanently, and humidity-lovers that failed before — a fern by the sink, for example — become realistic, because the missing ingredient has been supplied.

The Best Plants for a Windowless Bathroom (Rotation or Grow Light)

These are chosen for two qualities together: they tolerate low-light spells without sulking, and they are untroubled — often pleased — by bathroom humidity.

Pothos

The easiest recommendation. Pothos tolerates dim spells, trails or climbs to fit a shelf or the top of a cabinet, and is simple to propagate, so your "second plant" for the rotation can be a cutting of the first. Our pothos propagation guide shows the node-by-node method. Note that pothos is not a pet-safe plant; if animals share your flat, keep it out of reach and see our cat-safe houseplants guide for alternatives.

Snake plant

Upright, architectural, and unbothered by a week or two of dim bathroom life — it is one of the lowest-effort rotation plants there is. Its one bathroom caution is watering: in steamy air its soil dries slowly, and it would far rather be thirsty than wet. See our snake plant guide.

ZZ plant

Glossy, slow, and forgiving, the ZZ plant stores water in its roots and treats a bathroom rotation with indifference. Like the snake plant, it wants a light hand with the watering can in a humid room. Our ZZ plant guide covers its habits.

Spider plant

A natural for a bathroom shelf or a hanging spot, happy with humidity, and it produces baby plantlets you can pot up — which makes building a rotation pair free. Our spider plant guide covers the propagation.

Cast iron plant

Named for its toughness, this is a classic low-light plant with broad, dark leaves that suit a bathroom corner where a pot can sit on the floor. It grows slowly — buy the size you actually want — and asks for very little beyond the rotation or a light.

Plants to Avoid in a Windowless Bathroom (Unless You Add a Light)

  • Ferns, including Boston and maidenhair ferns. They love the humidity and are the internet's default bathroom answer — but they need real light to replace the fronds they shed, and in darkness they thin out steadily. With a grow light, the story changes completely, as the fern on a bathroom sink in our humidity coverage shows. Without one, rotate a fern only in short stays, and expect it to be the first plant to complain.
  • Calatheas and prayer plants. Humidity: perfect. Darkness: no. Their patterned leaves need decent light to stay strong; keep them in bright rooms and enjoy the bathroom for the plants above. See our prayer plant guide for what they actually ask for.
  • Herbs. Basil, mint, and friends are light-hungry outdoor plants being asked to live indoors; a windowless bathroom is the hardest room in the flat for them. A grow light can work — a dark shelf cannot.
  • Succulents and cacti. The opposite problem twice over: they want maximum light, and steamy air plus slow-drying soil works against the dry conditions they prefer.

The Other Bathroom Hazards Nobody Mentions

  • Cleaning products. Aerosol cleaners, bleach sprays, and air fresheners landing on leaves can mark or damage them. Move the plant out — or into the corridor — on cleaning day, and wipe leaves with a damp cloth if they get a film of spray or dust; clean leaves take in light better, which matters doubly here.
  • Soil that never dries. Steamy air slows evaporation, so a plant that drank weekly in the lounge may need water half as often in the bathroom. Always check before watering, and make sure the pot can drain — a decorative cover pot full of standing water is a quiet root problem. If small flies appear around persistently damp soil, our fungus gnat guide covers the fixes.
  • Cold draughts and extractor fans. A plant placed directly in the path of a cold draught or under a powerful extractor gets the worst of both. A shelf or the sink surround, out of the direct airflow, is a kinder spot.
  • Falling bottles and tight shelves. Bathrooms are small and elbows are real. A plant in a lightweight pot on a narrow ledge above a hard floor will eventually meet it. Choose a stable pot and a spot with margin around it.

Apartment Examples

The interior bathroom in a rented studio

No window, no permission to change fittings, one shelf above the sink. The tenant runs a two-plant pothos rotation: one plant trails from the bathroom shelf, its twin recovers on the bright kitchen shelf, swapped every Sunday with the laundry. No equipment, no cost beyond the second plant — which was a cutting of the first. Eighteen months on, both plants are still in the rotation, which is the point: the system, not any single plant, is what survives.

The family bathroom with a timer light

A small LED grow bulb in a lamp on a high shelf, on a plug timer, safely away from the shower. Under it, a fern lives by the sink full time — the plant that failed twice in this room before the light arrived. The bathroom routine did not change at all; the light simply does its hours while the house is empty.

Common Mistakes

  • Believing a "low light" label means "no light." Low light, in plant terms, means a room with a window but no direct sun — a north-facing room, a spot a few metres from glass. It has never meant a windowless room with the door shut.
  • Buying the fern first. It is the most recommended and the fastest to fade without light. Start with a pothos or snake plant rotation and learn your bathroom's real conditions.
  • Watering on the living-room schedule. Humid air slows drying; check the soil, every time.
  • Leaving the bathroom light on all day as the plant light. Ordinary bulbs used occasionally are not a growing light, and a ceiling light left burning for plants is an expensive, weak substitute for a small purpose-made LED on a timer.
  • Giving up after one death. A plant that faded in a dark bathroom was not proof you cannot keep plants — it was proof the room needed a system. Rotation or a light changes the result.

FAQ

Can any plant survive in a bathroom with no window?

Survive for a while, yes — most plants coast on stored reserves for days to weeks. Live there indefinitely and stay healthy, no: without a light source, every plant eventually declines. With a rotation into bright light, or a grow light on a timer, several common houseplants do well in a bathroom long term.

How often should I rotate bathroom plants?

Every one to two weeks works well in many flats as a starting rhythm. Watch the plant: if it comes out of the bathroom looking pale, stretched, or shedding leaves, shorten the stay; if it comes out unchanged, your rhythm is fine.

Is a grow light in a bathroom safe?

It can be, with equipment suited to a damp room, positioned away from splashes and the shower, and installed sensibly — and if you are in any doubt about fittings in a wet zone, an electrician's advice is the right step. Never improvise with equipment that is not meant for the room.

Why did my fern die in my windowless bathroom if ferns love bathrooms?

Because ferns love what a bathroom with a window offers — humidity plus light. Yours got the humidity and none of the light, so it slowly ran out of reserves. The same fern, rotated weekly or placed under a timed grow light, is a realistic bathroom plant.

What about artificial plants — is that the honest answer here?

For a truly dark bathroom where you do not want a rotation or a light, a good artificial plant is a perfectly reasonable choice, and a better one than a slow procession of real plants dying in the dark. This guide exists for readers who want a real plant — with the system that makes it fair on the plant.
