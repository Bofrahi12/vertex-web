Why Your Pothos Has Yellow Leaves: 7 Causes and Fixes

Few things are more discouraging than watching your pothos -- the plant everyone calls "unkillable" -- turn yellow leaf by leaf. The good news: yellow leaves on a pothos are almost never a death sentence. They are a message. The plant is telling you exactly what is wrong, and once you decode it, the fix is usually simple.

Below are the seven most common causes of yellow leaves on pothos, in roughly the order we see them, with how to diagnose each one and what to do about it.

First: A Quick Diagnosis Checklist

Before diving in, run through this 60-second check -- it solves most cases:

  1. Touch the soil. Soggy and smells musty? That points to overwatering. Bone-dry and pulling from the pot edges? That points to underwatering.
  2. Look at which leaves are yellow. Oldest leaves near the base only? Likely natural aging or underwatering. New growth yellowing? Nutrient or light problem. Random leaves everywhere? Overwatering or pests.
  3. Check the light. Is the plant suddenly in a much brighter or darker spot than before?
  4. Think about timing. Did the yellowing start after repotting, fertilizing, or a cold snap?

Keep your answers in mind as you read -- most yellowing comes down to one of the seven causes below.

Cause 1: Overwatering (The Number-One Culprit)

Overwatering causes more yellow pothos leaves than everything else combined. When soil stays constantly wet, roots cannot breathe, and they begin to rot. The plant responds by sacrificing leaves -- typically older ones first, turning yellow from the edges inward, often with a soft, limp feel rather than a crisp one.

How to confirm: The soil is wet days after watering, the pot feels heavy, and there may be a sour or musty smell. Yellow leaves feel soft and limp.

The fix: Let the soil dry out completely, then resume watering only when the top 2 inches are dry. If the plant is severely waterlogged, gently remove it from the pot, trim any black or mushy roots with clean scissors, and repot into fresh, well-draining soil. Make sure the pot has drainage holes -- a decorative pot with no holes is a slow-motion overwatering machine. Going forward, water thoroughly but infrequently: drench until water runs from the drainage holes, then do not water again until the top of the soil dries.

Cause 2: Underwatering

The mirror image of overwatering. A pothos left dry too long will yellow its oldest leaves first and drop them to conserve water. Unlike overwatering, underwatered yellow leaves feel dry, papery, and crispy, and the soil pulls away from the pot edges.

How to confirm: Soil is completely dry and shrunken, leaves feel crisp, and the plant perks up noticeably within hours of a good watering.

The fix: Water thoroughly right away -- place the pot in a sink and let water run through until it drains freely, which rehydrates shrunken soil that otherwise repels water. Then set a simple rhythm: check the top 2 inches of soil weekly, and water when dry. In most apartments that is every 1-2 weeks, less in winter.

Cause 3: Too Much Direct Sunlight

Pothos evolved as an understory vine, scrambling up trees in dappled shade. Several hours of harsh direct sun -- especially hot afternoon sun through a south or west window -- scorches the leaves. Sun damage looks distinctive: pale yellow or bleached patches, sometimes with brown crispy edges, on the leaves facing the window.

How to confirm: Yellowing or bleaching appears mainly on the window-facing side; leaves may feel thin and papery in the damaged spots.

The fix: Move the plant back a few feet from the window or add a sheer curtain. Bright indirect light is the sweet spot -- near a window, but out of the sun's direct path. Damaged leaves will not turn green again; you can trim the worst ones, and new growth will come in healthy.

Cause 4: Natural Aging (The One That Is Totally Fine)

Here is the cause nobody warns you about: pothos naturally shed their oldest leaves as they grow. If a single older leaf near the base of a vine turns yellow every few weeks while the rest of the plant looks vibrant and keeps producing new growth, that is not a problem -- it is the plant's normal lifecycle.

How to confirm: Only the oldest, lowest leaves are affected, one at a time, and new growth at the vine tips looks healthy and green.

The fix: None needed. Gently pull or snip off the spent leaf and carry on. Only worry if the rate accelerates -- several yellow leaves a week signals one of the other causes.

Cause 5: Nutrient Deficiency

A pothos that has been in the same soil for a year or more eventually exhausts the available nutrients. Nitrogen deficiency shows as a general pale yellowing of older leaves; the plant looks washed-out overall rather than having distinct yellow leaves among green ones. This is gradual -- it creeps up over months.

How to confirm: The whole plant looks pale, growth has slowed, and you have not fertilized or repotted in a year or more. (Rule out watering issues first -- they are far more common.)

The fix: Feed with a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at half the label strength, once a month during spring and summer. Do not fertilize in fall and winter when growth slows, and never fertilize a dry plant -- water first, then feed. If the soil is old and compacted, repotting into fresh potting mix in spring solves the deficiency and gives roots room at the same time.

Cause 6: Root-Bound Roots

A pothos that has been in the same pot for years can become so root-bound that there is barely any soil left -- just a dense brick of roots. Water runs straight through without being absorbed, so the plant effectively underwater itself no matter how often you water. Symptoms mimic underwatering: yellowing lower leaves, wilting even in moist-looking conditions, and stalled growth.

How to confirm: Roots are circling the drainage holes or pushing up through the soil surface; water drains through almost instantly; the plant dries out suspiciously fast after watering.

The fix: Repot into a container 1-2 inches wider, teasing the root ball gently loose. Spring is ideal, but a severely root-bound plant can be repotted any time -- it is already stressed, and fresh soil helps. Do not jump to a much bigger pot; excess empty soil stays wet and invites the overwatering problem from Cause 1.

Cause 7: Pests, Cold Drafts, or Temperature Stress

Less common, but worth checking when nothing else fits. Mealybugs (white cottony clusters) and spider mites (fine webbing, stippled leaves) both drain plant juices and cause yellowing. Cold drafts -- a pothos sitting against a drafty winter window or under an AC vent -- can yellow leaves almost overnight, since pothos dislike temperatures below about 55 F (13 C).

How to confirm: Inspect leaf joints and undersides closely for white fluff, tiny webs, or stippling. For cold damage, the yellowing follows a cold snap or the plant sits in a drafty spot.

The fix: For mealybugs, dab them with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, then rinse the plant. For spider mites, a strong shower plus wiping the leaves usually works for light infestations; repeat weekly. Move the plant away from cold windows and vents -- even a foot or two makes a difference.

Should You Remove the Yellow Leaves?

Yes. A yellow leaf will not turn green again -- the plant has already reclaimed what it can from it. Snip yellow leaves off at the base of their stem with clean scissors. This is not just cosmetic: removing spent leaves lets you see clearly whether the problem is continuing (new yellow leaves appearing means the cause is still active) or resolved.

One caution: do not strip the plant bare. If more than a third of the foliage is yellow, remove the worst leaves and fix the underlying cause first; the plant needs its remaining leaves to photosynthesize and recover.

Preventing Yellow Leaves: The 5-Minute Monthly Routine

Most pothos problems are prevented, not cured. Once a month, spend five minutes:

  1. Finger-test the soil and adjust your watering rhythm for the season.
  2. Wipe the leaves with a damp cloth -- dust blocks light and hides pests.
  3. Check the undersides of a few leaves for early pest signs.
  4. Rotate the pot a quarter-turn for even growth.
  5. Look at new growth. Healthy new leaves are the best proof your care is working.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can yellow pothos leaves turn green again?

No. Once a leaf has turned fully yellow, the chlorophyll is gone and it will not come back. The goal is always to stop new leaves from yellowing by fixing the cause. The plant itself recovers fine -- pothos push new growth readily once conditions improve.

How often should I water my pothos to avoid yellow leaves?

There is no calendar answer -- it depends on light, pot size, and season. The reliable method: stick your finger 2 inches into the soil. Dry? Water thoroughly. Still damp? Wait. In a typical apartment that is every 1-2 weeks in summer and every 2-3 weeks in winter. When in doubt, underwater slightly -- pothos tolerate dryness far better than sogginess.

Is it normal for pothos to lose leaves in winter?

Some increased leaf drop in winter is normal: shorter days mean less light, growth slows, and the plant sheds a few older leaves. What is not normal is rapid yellowing of many leaves at once, or new growth coming in yellow. If that is happening, work through the seven causes above -- winter just makes underlying watering mistakes show up faster.

My pothos is yellow AND leggy with long bare vines. What now?

Legginess plus yellowing usually means too little light combined with a watering issue. Fix the light first (move it closer to a window, out of direct sun), fix the watering, and then prune: cut the leggy vines back to a few inches above the soil, and root the cuttings in water. You will get a fuller plant plus free new ones -- pothos cuttings root in 1-2 weeks.

Final Thoughts

Yellow leaves on a pothos look alarming but are rarely fatal. In our experience, nine times out of ten the answer is watering -- usually too much, sometimes too little -- and the plant bounces back within weeks once the rhythm is right. Diagnose calmly, fix one thing at a time, and give it two or three weeks before judging the results. Your pothos is tougher than it looks.
