Bottom Watering Houseplants: When It Helps, When It Doesn't, and How to Do It in an Apartment

Sooner or later, every indoor gardener hears the same piece of advice, usually stated as if it were a law: you should be bottom watering. Set the pot in water, let the soil drink from below, never pour from above again. It sounds tidy, scientific, and kind to roots — and like most advice that arrives as a law, it is only partly true. Bottom watering is a genuinely useful technique for particular plants and particular problems. It is not a better way to water everything, and used on its own, all the time, it quietly creates a problem of its own.

Here is the short version before the detail: bottom watering works well when the soil has dried out so much that top watering runs straight through, when a plant hates water sitting on its leaves or crown, and when you want the soil surface to stay drier for a while. Top watering works better when the pot needs a proper flush, when the plant is large, and as the regular default for most healthy plants in free-draining soil. The routine that works well in many apartments uses both: bottom water when there is a reason, top water thoroughly every few waterings to wash the pot through. Results vary by soil mix, pot size, and the plant itself — which is exactly why the decision should be made per plant, not per ideology.

This guide covers the apartment version end to end: how bottom watering actually works, the honest case for and against it, a step-by-step routine sized for a kitchen sink or a washing-up bowl, how long to leave a pot soaking, which of the common apartment plants suit it and which do not, and the mistakes that turn a useful technique into soggy trouble.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • It is a tool, not a rule: bottom watering suits some plants and some situations. Most plants are perfectly happy being watered from the top, thoroughly, with the excess drained away.
  • Best reasons to bottom water: soil that has dried out and gone water-repellent, plants that dislike wet leaves or a wet crown (African violets and cyclamen are the classic apartment examples), and a spell of keeping the surface dry while you deal with fungus gnats.
  • The non-negotiable condition: the pot must have drainage holes, and the plant must be growing in its plain nursery or terracotta pot — a sealed decorative pot cannot bottom water. Lift it out of its cachepot first.
  • How long: until the surface of the soil just feels damp, often somewhere around 10 to 30 minutes for a typical small-to-medium pot. Then lift it out and let it drain. A pot left standing in water for hours is not being bottom watered; it is being drowned slowly.
  • It does not flush salts: water rising from below carries fertiliser and tap-water minerals up and leaves them in the soil. Keep a thorough top watering in the routine every few waterings to wash them out.
  • It does not prevent overwatering: a plant can be overwatered from below just as easily as from above. The check for whether a plant needs water at all does not change with the direction the water arrives from.

How Bottom Watering Actually Works

Bottom watering is simple capillary action — the same force that pulls water up a paper towel. You stand a pot with drainage holes in a shallow container of water, and the dry soil in the pot pulls moisture upwards through the holes, wetting the root ball from the bottom up. The drier the soil, the stronger the initial pull; as the soil becomes evenly moist, the movement slows and stops on its own.

Two practical consequences follow from that mechanism, and they explain both the strengths and the limits of the method:

  • It wets evenly, including the stubborn dry centre. Top watering a very dry pot often fails in a specific way: the shrunken root ball pulls away from the pot wall, and water runs down the gap and out of the holes while the middle stays dry. Water rising from below cannot take that shortcut — it is absorbed as it climbs. This is the single most reliable use of the technique, and it is the same rescue we recommend for a dried-out fern root ball.
  • It leaves the surface — and the leaves — dry. No water is poured over the top, so the upper layer of soil stays comparatively dry and nothing splashes onto foliage or sits in the crown of the plant. For plants that mark or rot when water sits on them, that matters. It is also why the method is often suggested during a fungus gnat problem: the surface layer the gnats breed in stays drier. Our guide to getting rid of fungus gnats covers the full routine — bottom watering is a supporting move in it, not the whole fix.

And one consequence in the other direction: because water only ever moves up, nothing is washed down and out. Whatever dissolved salts your tap water and fertiliser leave behind stay in the pot and concentrate near the surface over time. That is the honest trade at the centre of this whole article.

The Honest Case For — and Against

Where bottom watering genuinely helps

  • Re-wetting a pot that has dried out completely. A plant forgotten during a trip or a busy few weeks, in soil that now sheds water, is the clearest case. A soak from below re-wets the whole root ball in a way repeated top watering sometimes cannot. If the plant is drooping from thirst rather than from rot, this is a calm way to bring it back — the thirst-versus-rot check in our root rot guide is worth doing first, because soaking a plant whose roots have already rotted makes a bad situation worse.
  • Plants that dislike wet foliage or a wet crown. African violets are the textbook example: water on their fuzzy leaves and in the crown can mark and rot them, which is why our African violet care guide treats watering from below as the sensible default for that plant. Cyclamen have the same sensitivity at the crown — our cyclamen care guide makes the same point for a plant that is otherwise a perfect cool-windowsill winter flower.
  • Keeping the surface dry on purpose for a few weeks, for the fungus gnat reason above, or in a humid flat where the surface of a moisture-loving plant's pot is growing a film of algae or mould that you would rather discourage.
  • Small pots and propagation. Little pots of cuttings and young plants dry unevenly and are easily washed out of shape by a heavy pour from above. A shallow tray soak waters a whole group of them gently at once — handy if you are rooting cuttings the way we describe in our pothos propagation guide and potting them up in batches.

Where top watering is the better tool

  • Flushing the pot. Every plant that is fed, and every plant watered with hard tap water, slowly accumulates salts in its soil. The white crust that forms on terracotta and on soil surfaces is the visible part of it. The only way out is through: a thorough top watering, with plenty of water running freely out of the drainage holes, carrying the excess with it. No bottom-watering routine replaces this.
  • Large and tall plants. A big rubber plant or monstera in a heavy pot is not going into your sink, and a shallow tray under it holds too little water to wet a deep root ball. For the blog's larger plants — the rubber plant is a good example — careful, thorough top watering in place, with the saucer emptied afterwards, is the practical method.
  • Plants that want a real drying cycle. Snake plants, ZZ plants, and other drought-storing plants want their soil to dry substantially and then be watered deeply and drained — a soak-and-dry rhythm from the top. Our snake plant guide is built around exactly that. Bottom watering these plants tends to keep the lower soil wet for longer, which works against the dry rest their roots expect.
  • A quick, ordinary watering day. For a healthy plant in an open, free-draining mix, top watering thoroughly takes seconds and wets the whole pot. There is no prize for making it slower.

How to Bottom Water, Step by Step (Apartment Edition)

You do not need any special equipment. In a flat, the realistic soaking stations are the kitchen sink, a washing-up bowl, a deep tray or baking dish, or the bath for a bigger batch. Work on a towel if you are doing this on a counter or the floor, and keep the whole thing close to the sink — carrying dripping pots across a room is how floors and shelves get water marks.

  1. Check the plant actually needs water. Bottom watering changes the direction, not the decision. Use the same check you would use for any watering — the finger test and the weight of the pot, as set out in our guide to how often to water indoor plants. If the soil is still moist, put the plant back. Soaking an already wet plant is overwatering with extra steps.
  2. Take the plant out of its decorative pot. Bottom watering happens in the pot with drainage holes. If the plant usually sits inside a cachepot, lift the inner pot out — and check the cachepot is not already holding old water while you are there.
  3. Fill your sink, bowl, or tray with room-temperature water, a few centimetres deep — enough to come partway up the side of the pot, not over the rim. Cold water straight from a winter tap is an unnecessary shock; water you have let stand in the watering can is ideal.
  4. Stand the pot in the water and wait. For most small-to-medium pots, check after about 10 minutes and then every few minutes after that. You are waiting for one specific sign: the surface of the soil just turning damp and slightly darker. That is the moment the whole root ball is wet.
  5. Lift the pot out and let it drain fully — in the empty sink, on the draining board, or back in the bowl tipped empty — until drips have more or less stopped. This step is not optional tidiness; it is what separates bottom watering from leaving a plant standing in water.
  6. Put the plant back in its spot, and empty the decorative pot or saucer of anything that collects later. Note, even mentally, which plants were bottom watered this time, so the rotation in the next section actually happens.

A batch tip for small flats: line the plants up by the sink, soak them in size order (smallest first — they finish fastest), and drain them on a towel while the next group soaks. A whole shelf of small pots can be done in one sink of water, and the floor stays dry. Change the water if you are soaking a plant you suspect has a pest or a problem, so you are not giving everything else a bath in the same water.

How Long Is Long Enough?

There is no single correct number of minutes, because the soak time depends on the size of the pot, how dry the soil was, and what the mix is made of. A fine, peat- or coir-based mix wicks water up noticeably faster than a chunky, bark-heavy mix, which can be slow work from below. Use these as practical starting points, and let the surface check overrule all of them:

  • Small pots (roughly up to 10 cm): often done in about 10 to 15 minutes.
  • Medium pots (roughly 12 to 17 cm): often somewhere around 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Large pots: bottom watering becomes impractical before it becomes complete — the water has a long way to climb. Top water these instead.

The surface test is the real rule: when the top of the soil feels just damp to a fingertip, the pot is done, whether that took 8 minutes or 35. And there is a ceiling as well as a target — if a pot has been standing in water for the better part of an hour and the surface is still dry, something else is going on (a very barky mix, blocked drainage holes, or a root ball so dry it needs a longer, patient rescue soak with the water refreshed). What it does not need is to be left in the sink and forgotten until evening. Roots need air as well as water, and a pot standing in water is a pot whose roots are running out of air — the road to the problems in the root rot guide linked above.

Which Plants Suit It: A Decision List

Rather than a verdict for every species, here is the pattern, with the apartment plants this blog covers most often sorted by it:

Usually a good fit for bottom watering, at least some of the time

  • African violets — the strongest case on the list, for the dry-leaves, dry-crown reason.
  • Cyclamen — same crown sensitivity, especially while in flower.
  • Ferns and other evenly-moist plants in small-to-medium pots — bottom watering helps correct the dry-centre problem, as long as the pot still comes out to drain.
  • Peace lilies — a thirsty, forgiving plant that takes a soak well when it has been allowed to get properly dry; our peace lily guide covers its very clear thirst signal, the droop, which tells you when a soak is actually needed.
  • Cuttings, seedlings, and small newly potted plants — gentle, even wetting without disturbance.

Usually better watered from the top

  • Snake plants, ZZ plants, and other drought-storing plants — they want a deep drink and then a real dry period, not a prolonged wet base.
  • Large floor plants — impractical to soak, and their deep root balls wet more reliably from above.
  • Anything in a chunky, bark-based mix (many orchids are the extreme case) — capillary action is weak in a very open mix, so a soak may wet the bottom and leave the top half dry. Our orchid care guide describes the dunk-and-drain approach orchids in bark actually respond to, which is a different technique again.
  • Any plant due a flush — which, in rotation, is every plant.

Notice what is not on either list: a plant that must only ever be watered one way. With very few exceptions, the method is a choice you make per watering, based on what that plant needs that week.

The Rotation That Makes It Work Long-Term

If you take one system from this article, take this one, because it captures the benefits of bottom watering without importing its one real drawback:

  • Bottom water when there is a reason — a dried-out pot, a wet-shy plant, a gnat campaign, a tray of cuttings.
  • Top water thoroughly every few waterings — enough water to run freely out of the drainage holes for a short while, then drain the saucer. This is the flush that carries accumulated salts out of the pot. If your tap water is hard, or you feed regularly, lean towards the more frequent end of "every few".
  • Keep the need-check identical for both. Direction never decides whether to water; dryness does. The moisture-sensing habits in our guide to plant care apps and moisture sensors apply in exactly the same way whichever end of the pot the water enters.

A useful sign that the flush is overdue: a white, crusty deposit building up on the soil surface or around the rim of a terracotta pot, or a plant that is watered and fed sensibly but develops brown leaf tips anyway. Flush first, then look for other causes.

Bottom Watering Away From Home — and Other Apartment Realities

A question that comes up often: can you leave plants standing in a tray of water while you go on holiday, as a slow self-watering system? As a short emergency measure for a few days, a shallow tray can keep a thirsty plant from drying out completely — but it is a compromise, not a system, and for anything longer it risks exactly the standing-water root problems this article has been warning about. The timed, plant-by-plant plans in our vacation watering guide — for 3, 7, and 14 days away — are the safer way to handle a trip.

Two other flat-specific notes. First, shared saucers and trays share problems: a plant with fungus gnats or a root problem soaked in the same water as healthy plants can pass trouble along, so soak suspect plants separately and refresh the water. Second, protect your surfaces: a pot that has been soaked is heavier and damper than you expect, and terracotta in particular will weep moisture into a wooden shelf. Let everything drain properly, and keep a saucer or mat under pots returned to wood or fabric-adjacent spots. If a pot has no drainage holes at all and that is why you are improvising, the longer-term fix is a repot into a proper pot — our guide to repotting a houseplant in an apartment covers doing that without covering the flat in soil.

Common Mistakes

  • Leaving the pot in the water and forgetting it. The most common bottom-watering failure by a wide margin. Set a timer if you are the forgetful type; the surface check takes seconds.
  • Bottom watering a plant that did not need water. Direction is not a safeguard. A wet plant soaked again is an overwatered plant, however gently the water arrived.
  • Never top watering at all. Months of exclusive bottom watering concentrate salts at the surface — the white crust and the unexplained brown tips are the plant's report on it. Keep the flush in the rotation.
  • Trying to bottom water through a decorative pot. No holes, no capillary action — the plant just sits in a puddle inside a sealed pot, which is the worst version of both methods.
  • Soaking everything in one bath, including the sick plant. Isolate problem plants, refresh the water, and do not let a rescue soak become a shared soak.
  • Judging the soak by the clock instead of the surface. Mixes and pot sizes vary too much for a fixed number of minutes to be a rule. Damp surface, out, drain — in that order.

Quick Questions, Straight Answers

Is bottom watering better than top watering?

Neither is better in general — they are good at different jobs. Bottom watering is better at re-wetting a dried-out root ball evenly and at keeping leaves, crowns, and the soil surface dry. Top watering is better at flushing salts out of the pot and at watering large plants. A routine that uses both, chosen per plant and per week, works well in many apartments.

How long should I leave a plant bottom watering?

Until the surface of the soil just feels damp — often around 10 to 15 minutes for a small pot and 20 to 30 minutes for a medium one, longer or shorter depending on the mix. Check with a fingertip rather than trusting a fixed time, then lift the pot out and let it drain completely. Do not leave it standing in water for hours.

Can you overwater a plant by bottom watering?

Yes. The roots cannot tell which direction the water came from; soil that stays wet for too long deprives them of air in exactly the same way. Check that the plant needs water before you soak it, and always let the pot drain afterwards.

Does bottom watering help with fungus gnats?

It often helps as part of a wider routine, because it lets the surface layer of soil — where gnats lay eggs and the larvae live — stay drier between waterings. On its own it is rarely the whole answer; letting the soil dry appropriately, removing dying leaves, and catching the adults matter just as much.

Which plants should not be bottom watered?

Plants that want a pronounced dry period — snake plants, ZZ plants, and similar drought-storing plants — are usually happier with a thorough top watering followed by a real dry rest. Large plants and plants in very chunky, bark-based mixes are also poor candidates, for practical and capillary reasons respectively.

Why is there a white crust on my soil since I started bottom watering?

Most likely mineral and fertiliser salts carried up by the rising water and left behind at the surface as it evaporates. It is the signature drawback of watering only from below. A thorough top watering that runs freely out of the drainage holes flushes much of it away — build that flush into your routine every few waterings.

Can I bottom water a plant in a pot with no drainage holes?

No — the method depends entirely on water entering through the drainage holes. A pot without holes needs repotting into one that has them, ideally with the decorative pot kept as an outer cachepot that the plant is lifted out of for watering.
