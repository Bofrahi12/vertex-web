How to Get Rid of Fungus Gnats: 5 Methods That Actually Work

They are tiny, they are everywhere, and they have chosen your favorite plant as their nursery. Fungus gnats — those small black flies hovering around your pots — are the most common indoor plant pest, and the most annoying: harmless to you, maddening to live with, and a sign that something in your soil is off.

Here is the honest version: there is no single spray that fixes this. Fungus gnats have a two-week life cycle split between flying adults and soil-dwelling larvae, so you have to attack both stages at once. The good news: the methods below are ranked by what actually works, and the top two together solve the vast majority of infestations within a month.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • What they are: tiny black flies; the larvae live in damp soil and feed on fungi and fine roots.
  • The combo that works: yellow sticky traps (catches adults) + mosquito bits / BTI (kills larvae) — use both, not one.
  • The free fix: let the top 2 inches of soil dry out between waterings — gnats cannot breed in dry soil. Our finger test guide shows how.
  • Timeline: expect 3–4 weeks — you are breaking a breeding cycle, not winning a battle.
  • The honest catch: if you only kill the flying adults, the larvae in the soil replace them within days.

Confirm It Is Actually Fungus Gnats

Before treating, make sure. Fungus gnats are:

  • Tiny (2–4 mm), dark, mosquito-like flies that hover around the soil surface and run across it — they are weak fliers and rarely go far from the pot.
  • Most active when you water or disturb the plant — a small cloud rising from the soil is the classic sign.
  • Not fruit flies — fruit flies hover around kitchens and drains, are rounder and tanner, and do not care about your plants.

One more check: press a slice of raw potato onto the soil surface for 48 hours, then look at the underside. Tiny translucent worm-like larvae with black heads confirm a breeding population. This is the cheapest diagnostic in plant care.

Why They Showed Up

Fungus gnats do not appear from nowhere — they arrive as eggs in new potting soil or on a new plant, then explode when conditions suit them. The conditions are almost always the same:

Soil that stays wet. The larvae need constant moisture and the fungi that grow in it. If your pots never dry out on top, you have built a gnat nursery. Overwatering is the #1 cause — which is why the finger test is pest control, not just plant care.

Dense, peaty soil. Cheap potting mixes stay soggy and break down into exactly the organic mush larvae love. A chunkier mix with perlite or bark dries faster on top.

No drainage. Saucers full of standing water and pots without holes keep the bottom of the pot permanently wet — prime real estate for larvae.

They peak in fall and winter, when lower light slows evaporation and heated indoor air keeps things cozy. If your gnat problem started in October, that is why.

The 5 Methods, Ranked by What Actually Works

1. Mosquito bits (BTI) — kills the larvae

The single most effective treatment. Mosquito bits contain Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (BTI), a naturally occurring bacterium lethal to fly larvae and harmless to plants, pets, and people. Soak the bits in your watering can for 30 minutes, then water as normal — the larvae die, the cycle breaks. Repeat for 2–3 waterings. This targets the actual problem: the next generation.

2. Yellow sticky traps — catches the adults

Those little yellow cards on sticks look silly and work brilliantly. Fungus gnats are drawn to yellow; the traps catch the egg-laying adults before they can breed. They do not solve the larvae already in the soil — which is why traps alone feel like they "don't work." Paired with BTI, they are the one-two punch: BTI kills the young, traps catch the breeders.

3. Let the soil dry out — the free fix

Allow the top 2 inches of soil to dry completely between waterings. Gnat eggs and larvae desiccate in dry soil; adults will not lay eggs where the surface is dry. This alone can collapse a mild infestation — and it is also just correct watering for most houseplants. Check moisture with the finger test rather than guessing.

4. Hydrogen peroxide drench — the quick knockdown

A 1:4 mix of 3% hydrogen peroxide to water, poured through the soil, kills larvae on contact and fizzes satisfyingly while doing it. It works — once. It also kills beneficial soil microbes and can stress roots with repeated use, so treat it as emergency triage, not a routine. One drench, then switch to BTI and drying for the long game.

5. Repotting in fresh mix — the nuclear option

If a plant is badly infested, sometimes the fastest fix is evicting everyone: remove the plant, shake off all old soil, rinse the roots, and repot in fresh, sterile mix in a clean pot. It is disruptive — the plant will sulk for a week or two — but it removes the entire breeding population at once. Reserve this for severe cases or when you were going to repot anyway. Our winter plant care guide notes why fall repotting needs extra care with watering afterward.

What about sand or gravel top layers? A half-inch of horticultural sand or fine gravel on the soil surface does deter egg-laying — adults cannot easily reach the soil. It works as a supplement, not a cure: larvae already below keep developing. Fine to add; do not rely on it alone.

Prevention: Keep Them From Coming Back

Killing the current infestation is half the job. The other half is making your pots inhospitable:

  • Water from the top inch, not the calendar. The finger test, every time. Most reinfestations trace back to a plant that quietly went back to soggy.
  • Use chunky, fast-draining mix. Perlite, orchid bark, or pumice in the mix means the surface dries between waterings.
  • Empty saucers. Standing water under the pot is a swamp nursery. Empty them 30 minutes after watering.
  • Quarantine new plants. Keep newcomers away from the collection for 2 weeks and watch the soil. Many infestations arrive home in the nursery pot.
  • Bottom-water when practical. Watering from the saucer keeps the top inch — where gnats lay eggs — dry while roots drink from below. Not suitable for every plant, but excellent for gnat-prone ones.

Are fungus gnats harmful to plants?

To established houseplants, barely — the larvae nibble fine root hairs and fungi, which healthy plants tolerate. Seedlings and cuttings are the exception: tiny root systems can be seriously damaged. The real harm is to your sanity.

Will they go away on their own in winter?

No — indoor heating keeps them breeding year-round. They may slow down if you water less, but the population persists in damp soil until you break the cycle deliberately.

Can I just use apple cider vinegar traps?

Those catch fruit flies, not fungus gnats. Gnat adults are weak fliers drawn to yellow and to soil — a vinegar jar across the room catches almost none of them. Yellow sticky traps placed at soil level are the correct trap.

How long until they are gone?

Plan on 3–4 weeks of consistent treatment. That is two full life cycles — enough for the BTI to kill successive larval generations while traps remove the adults. If you are still seeing clouds after a month, something is still too wet: recheck every pot, including the one you forgot behind the curtain.

Final Thoughts

Fungus gnats feel like an invasion, but they are really a message: something in your watering or soil is staying too wet. The fix — BTI plus sticky traps plus drier soil — works on nearly every infestation, and the prevention is just good plant care you should be doing anyway.

Do not chase the perfect single product. Break the cycle at both ends, give it a month, and fix the moisture that invited them in. Your plants were trying to tell you about the water long before the flies showed up.
