How to Get Rid of Mealybugs on Houseplants: Isolate, Dab, Repeat

White fluff on a houseplant is easy to walk past. It looks like a scrap of cotton wool caught on a stem, or a dusting of flour where a leaf joins the plant — until you look closely and the fluff has legs. Those are mealybugs: small, soft-bodied insects covered in a white waxy coating that makes them look like tiny bits of cotton. They feed on plant sap, they hide in the joints and undersides you rarely inspect, and in a flat — where plants crowd together on one windowsill or shelf — they rarely stay on just one plant.

The treatment itself is not complicated, and it does not start with buying anything. It starts with moving the plant away from the others, then dabbing every visible bug with alcohol on a cotton swab, then repeating on a schedule, because the eggs and the tiny young stages survive the first pass. This guide walks through confirming what you have found, the full treatment step by step at a sink or in a bathtub, how long to keep repeating, and how to check the neighbouring plants that shared the shelf. Results vary by how early you catch them and how thoroughly you repeat, but a light infestation caught early often clears completely with swabs, patience, and isolation.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • What you are looking at: soft white or pale grey insects, a few millimetres long, clustered where leaves join stems, along leaf veins, and on undersides — often with cottony white fluff and sticky leaves nearby.
  • First move, before any treatment: move the plant away from every other plant. A bathroom or a spare corner is your quarantine station. Mealybugs spread by crawling between touching leaves.
  • The core treatment: dip a cotton swab in rubbing alcohol (the standard 70% isopropyl bottle) and dab each bug and cluster directly. The alcohol breaks down the waxy coating on contact. Test on one small area first and wait a day if the plant is delicate or fuzzy-leaved.
  • One treatment is never the whole job. Eggs are protected inside the cottony masses and young mealybugs are tiny and pale. Re-check and re-treat every few days at first, then weekly, until several weeks pass with nothing new appearing.
  • Check the neighbours. Every plant that shared the shelf or touched the infested plant gets a close inspection, joints and undersides included — even if it looks clean.
  • Sticky leaves and black sooty patches are side effects of feeding (a sugary residue the bugs leave behind), and they wash off with a damp cloth once the bugs are gone.
  • The honest line: a small, cheap plant that is infested in every joint, roots included, is sometimes better replaced than treated for months next to a healthy collection.

First, Confirm It Is Mealybugs (Not Mould, Dust, or Something Else)

Several white things appear on houseplants, and the treatment differs, so spend two minutes confirming before you start dabbing.

  • Mealybugs are individual insects: oval, segmented, soft, white to pale pinkish-grey, and they sit in clusters in protected spots — leaf joints, stem crotches, the underside midrib of a leaf, tight new growth. Touch one with a fingertip or swab and it is a body, not a smear. The cottony fluff around a cluster is wax and egg mass, not webbing.
  • Mould or mildew smears and spreads across a surface, usually on soil or a dying leaf, and has no individual bodies in it. White mould on the soil surface is a dampness issue, not this one.
  • Woolly aphids and scale are the close lookalikes. Scale insects are flatter, harder, dome- or shell-like bumps that do not look cottony; they are treated similarly (alcohol dabbing often helps there too), but they do not produce the fluffy egg masses. If it is fluffy and clustered, mealybugs are the likely answer.
  • Fungus gnats are not this. If your problem is tiny black flies around the soil rather than white clusters on the plant itself, that is a different pest with a different fix — see our fungus gnats guide instead.

Two supporting clues strengthen the diagnosis: sticky leaves (the sugary residue mealybugs excrete as they feed, sometimes with a thin black sooty film growing on it) and a plant that is yellowing, dropping leaves, or stalling despite normal watering. Neither clue alone proves mealybugs — but white clusters plus stickiness is about as clear as houseplant diagnosis gets.

Where They Hide: The Two-Minute Inspection

Mealybugs are masters of the spot you did not check. Before treating, inspect the whole plant slowly, ideally over a sink or bathtub so anything you dislodge is contained:

  1. Every leaf joint and stem crotch — the classic colony site, especially on pothos, philodendron, ficus, jade plants, succulents, orchids, and citrus.
  2. Leaf undersides, along the central vein and in any fold or curl.
  3. Tight new growth at the tips, where several young leaves are folded together.
  4. The pot itself: under the rim, around drainage holes, and between the nursery pot and a decorative cover pot — colonies are often found living on the pot, off the plant entirely.
  5. The roots, if the plant keeps declining despite clean foliage: a less common root-feeding type lives in the soil. If the top of the plant is clean but it still stalls and the soil stays oddly dry-looking at the edges, unpot it once and look for white waxy clusters on the roots and the inside of the pot.

The Treatment: Step by Step at the Sink

You need: cotton swabs, rubbing alcohol (70% isopropyl), a soft cloth, and optionally a spray bottle and a ready-to-use insecticidal soap or neem spray made for houseplants. Work in the sink, bathtub, or shower tray — in a flat, this is your washable treatment station, and it keeps dislodged bugs and spray off your floors and furniture.

Step 1: Isolate first, treat second

Move the plant away from the collection before you touch it — carrying a dripping, freshly disturbed plant past the others defeats the point. If several plants were touching it, assume the neighbours need inspecting today too (Step 5). Keep the treated plant in quarantine for the whole treatment period, not just the first day.

Step 2: Dab every visible bug with alcohol

Dip a cotton swab in the alcohol and press it directly onto each bug and cluster for a few seconds. The waxy coating that protects mealybugs from most sprays is exactly what alcohol dissolves, which is why this simple step does the heavy lifting on visible insects. Work joint by joint, underside by underside, and re-dip the swab constantly — a dry swab just moves bugs around. Treated bugs typically darken as they die; wipe them off with the swab or cloth as you go so you can see what is left. Two cautions: test first on one small, hidden area and check the next day if the plant is thin-leaved, fuzzy-leaved, or a succulent you care about, and keep alcohol on the bugs rather than soaking whole leaves.

Step 3: Wash the plant down

After dabbing, rinse the plant gently in the shower or with a sink sprayer — lukewarm water, modest pressure. Indoors, this replaces the garden-hose blast outdoor guides rely on, and it washes off dead bugs, eggs dislodged by dabbing, and the sticky residue. Let it drain fully before it goes back to its quarantine spot, and empty any saucer — a rescued plant standing in water invites a different problem, as our root rot guide explains.

Step 4: Follow up with a spray only if the infestation is more than light

For a handful of clusters, dabbing plus washing is often enough, repeated as below. For a plant with colonies in most joints, a thorough spray of insecticidal soap (or a neem product labelled for houseplants) reaches the crevices a swab cannot. Whichever product you use, follow its label directions, cover undersides and joints until they are wet, apply it out of direct sun, and test on a small area first if the plant is delicate. Sprays work on contact and on the young stages they touch — they do not reliably reach eggs inside the cottony masses, which is why the next step matters more than the product choice.

Step 5: Inspect and treat the neighbours

Every plant that shared the shelf, windowsill, or trailing contact gets the two-minute inspection from the section above — joints, undersides, pot rims. Dab anything you find, and keep the whole group under weekly observation while treatment continues. Crowded shelves are the apartment-specific reason mealybugs become a collection problem instead of a one-plant problem: leaves that touch are bridges.

Step 6: Repeat on a calendar, not on a hunch

This is the step most people skip, and the reason mealybugs "come back": the first session removes the visible adults, while eggs and tiny pale crawlers — easy to miss at a few days old — are still developing. A practical rhythm that works well in many apartments:

  • Days 1–7: inspect every 2–3 days; dab anything new immediately.
  • Weeks 2–4: inspect and treat weekly, including the neighbours.
  • All-clear: only after several consecutive checks with nothing new — no clusters, no new fluff, no fresh stickiness — does the plant leave quarantine and return to the shelf.

Judge progress by new growth: clean, normal new leaves are the real sign the colony is finished. Old damaged leaves will not repair themselves, and removing the worst-hit leaves or a heavily infested stem tip as you go is reasonable — bag prunings and put them in the household bin, not in any indoor compost.

When Dabbing Is Not Enough: Prune, Repot, or Let Go

  • Prune the worst growth. If one stem tip or leaf cluster holds most of the colony, cutting it off removes bugs and egg masses in one move and makes the rest of the plant treatable. Cut back to healthy growth with clean scissors.
  • Repot when the pot or roots are involved. If you found clusters under the rim, between the pots, or on the roots, tip the plant out in the bathtub, shake and wash off the old mix, wipe or wash the pot thoroughly (or use a fresh one), and repot into fresh mix — our apartment repotting guide covers the mess-free bathtub method step by step. Keep the plant quarantined after repotting.
  • Let go, honestly. A plant that is infested in every joint after weeks of thorough treatment — especially an inexpensive, easily replaced one — puts the rest of a flat's collection at risk every day it stays. Replacing it is not failure; it is protecting the other plants. Sentimental or rare plants are worth the long campaign; a supermarket succulent usually is not.

Keeping Them Out: Prevention for a Small Collection

  • Quarantine every new plant. The most common way mealybugs enter a flat is on a new purchase. Two or three weeks on its own — a bathroom shelf works — with a weekly joint-and-underside inspection before it joins the shelf prevents most introductions.
  • Inspect when you water. A ten-second check of joints and undersides while you are already holding the watering can catches colonies while they are still a two-swab problem. The checking habit in our watering guide pairs naturally with this.
  • Give plants a little air and space. Persistently crowded, touching foliage in still air is where colonies build unnoticed. You do not need a sparse shelf — just leaves that are not pressed together in a permanent tangle, and a wipe-down of leaves now and then, which also removes the sticky residue early colonies leave.
  • Be careful with treatment products around pets. Dabbing alcohol on a plant is a small, targeted use, but keep treated plants away from cats and dogs until leaves are dry, store bottles out of reach, and follow label guidance for any spray. If pet safety shapes which plants you keep at all, our cat-safe houseplants guide covers the plant side of that decision.

Apartment Examples

The pothos trailing into its neighbours

A pothos on a shared shelf trails across a philodendron and a fern. White clusters appear in the pothos joints — and because the vines touch, the philodendron has two small colonies starting at the contact points, while the fern is clean. Treatment: pothos quarantined in the bathtub for the full repeat cycle, philodendron dabbed and watched weekly, shelf wiped down, and the pothos pruned back so it no longer bridges to its neighbours when it returns.

The new orchid that brought guests

A flowering orchid arrives from the shop and goes straight onto the main windowsill. A week later there is fluff deep in a leaf joint and at the base of the flower spike. Caught this early, it is a swab-and-isolate job: dab each cluster, move the orchid to a separate room, inspect the two plants it sat between, and check weekly. The lesson is the quarantine rule — the same plant inspected alone for its first weeks would never have put the windowsill collection at risk.

Common Mistakes

  • Spraying once and declaring victory. Visible adults die; eggs inside the cottony masses hatch later. The repeat calendar, not the product, is what finishes an infestation.
  • Treating the plant but not checking its neighbours. In a crowded flat collection the colony has usually already sent crawlers across touching leaves.
  • Moving the plant back to the shelf after the first clean. Quarantine lasts until several consecutive checks come back clean.
  • Soaking whole leaves in alcohol instead of dabbing the bugs. Alcohol on the insect is the treatment; alcohol across delicate or fuzzy foliage can mark or damage it. Test first, dab precisely.
  • Ignoring the pot. Colonies under the rim and between the nursery pot and cover pot survive a perfect leaf treatment and simply walk back.
  • Scrubbing off the fluff and nothing else. Removing the cottony wax without treating the insects underneath leaves the colony alive and harder to see.

FAQ

Are mealybugs harmful to people or pets?

Mealybugs feed on plants, not people or pets, and they do not bite. The practical cautions are about the treatments, not the insect: keep alcohol and any spray products stored safely, let treated leaves dry before a pet can chew them, and follow the label on anything you spray indoors.

Why do mealybugs keep coming back after I treat them?

Almost always because of timing or coverage: eggs protected inside the cottony masses hatch after the first treatment, a colony on the pot or a neighbouring plant was missed, or the plant went back to the shelf too soon. A weekly inspect-and-dab cycle for several weeks, neighbours included, addresses all three causes.

Can I just wash mealybugs off with water?

A shower rinse removes loose bugs and residue and is a useful part of the routine, but on its own it rarely finishes the job: the waxy coating sheds water, and clusters wedged in joints survive a rinse. Dabbing with alcohol is what breaks the coating — washing supports it, it does not replace it.

What is the sticky stuff on the leaves (and the black film)?

Feeding mealybugs excrete a sugary residue that coats leaves and surfaces below the plant; a dark sooty mould commonly grows on that residue. Both wash off with a damp cloth once the insects are gone, and neither returns if the colony is actually finished — fresh stickiness on a "treated" plant is a sign to inspect again.

Should I throw the plant away?

Only in the heavy, persistent case: colonies in most joints and on the roots after weeks of careful treatment, on a plant that is easy to replace. Light and moderate cases on a healthy plant usually clear with dabbing, washing, and the repeat cycle — the decision is about risk to the rest of your collection, not about one bad week.

Do mealybugs live in the soil?

Most houseplant mealybugs live on stems, joints, and leaves, but a root-feeding type does live in the pot. Suspect it if a plant declines with a clean top: unpot it, and look for white waxy clusters on the roots and pot walls. Washing the roots, removing the old mix, and repotting into fresh mix is the treatment there.

Finding mealybugs feels worse than it usually is. The insects are slow, visible, and vulnerable to the simplest tool in the bathroom cabinet — what they rely on is being ignored in the joints and undersides, and being treated once instead of on a schedule. Isolate the plant, dab what you can see, wash it down, check the neighbours, and keep the weekly appointment until several clean checks in a row say it is finished. That routine, done patiently in a sink or bathtub, clears most apartment infestations — and the two-minute inspection that found them becomes part of how you water from now on.
