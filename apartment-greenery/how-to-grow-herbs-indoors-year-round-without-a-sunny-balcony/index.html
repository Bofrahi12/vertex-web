How to Grow Herbs Indoors Year-Round (Without a Sunny Balcony)

Fresh basil in January sounds like a luxury reserved for people with greenhouses. It is not — but the herb kits sold in supermarkets come with an unspoken lie: that a tiny pot on a dark kitchen shelf will feed you forever. Most of them die within weeks, and the buyer blames themselves instead of the kit.

Here is the honest version: a handful of herbs genuinely thrive indoors year-round with nothing fancier than a bright window and basic care. A few others are seasonal drama queens that will frustrate you. This guide separates the two, then gives you the light, water, and harvesting rules that make a small apartment herb garden actually productive.

The 30-Second Herb Garden Cheat Sheet

  • Start with: chives, thyme, mint, oregano — the forgiving four that tolerate apartment life.
  • Light: 6+ hours of direct sun (south or west window), or a LED grow light 6–8 inches above the leaves.
  • Water: Finger-test rule — top inch dry means water thoroughly; most herbs die from soggy pots, not thirst.
  • Pots: One plant per 6-inch pot minimum, with drainage holes. Crowded supermarket pots need dividing on day one.
  • Harvest rule: Never take more than one-third of the plant at once.
  • The honest part: Indoor basil is a winter diva — buy the cheap supermarket pot in summer, grow the hardy herbs through winter.

Pick Your Winners: The Best Indoor Herbs, Ranked by Forgiveness

Not all herbs are equal indoors. Here is the ranking that matters for apartment growers:

Tier 1 — Genuinely easy (start here):

  • Chives. Nearly unkillable. Tolerate lower light, bounce back from neglect, and regrow fast after cutting. The perfect first herb.
  • Thyme. Loves bright light, hates wet feet. Once established, it forgives missed waterings better than almost anything.
  • Mint. So vigorous it is invasive outdoors — which makes it perfect indoors. The catch: give it its own pot. Mint will strangle anything planted beside it.
  • Oregano. Similar to thyme: sun-lover, drought-tolerant, and it actually tastes better when slightly stressed.

Tier 2 — Doable with good light:

  • Parsley. Slow to start (seeds can take 3 weeks to sprout), but a healthy plant produces for months. Likes consistent moisture — the one herb where you water a bit more generously.
  • Cilantro (coriander). The sprinter: grows fast, bolts fast. Sow new seeds every 2–3 weeks for a continuous supply, because one plant will not last.
  • Rosemary. Gorgeous and fragrant, but the classic indoor casualty. It wants bright light, cool nights, and humidity — winter apartments give it none of these. Grow it outdoors in summer, bring it in as a project, not a staple.

Tier 3 — The winter diva:

  • Basil. Everyone's favorite, and the number-one indoor herb failure. It needs serious light and warmth, sulks below 50°F (10°C), and gets leggy fast without strong sun. In summer on a sunny windowsill it is glorious. From November to March, even experienced growers struggle. No shame in buying supermarket basil in winter — that is what it is for.

Light: The One Thing You Cannot Fake (Much)

Herbs are sun plants. Most want 6 or more hours of direct sunlight daily — more than most houseplants. Your sunniest window is a south or west-facing one; an east window works for the forgiving tier but slows growth.

The window test: Put your hand between the window and where the pot sits. If you can feel real warmth on a sunny day, the spot works. If the light feels like vague brightness, it is not enough for herbs — fine for a low-light houseplant, not for basil.

Grow lights are the honest fix. A basic full-spectrum LED panel 6–8 inches above the herbs, on 12–14 hours a day, turns any dark kitchen into a herb garden. This is the single purchase that separates thriving indoor herb growers from frustrated ones — cheaper than replacing dead supermarket pots every month.

Rotate weekly. Herbs lean hard toward the light. A quarter-turn every few days keeps growth even instead of lopsided.

Pots, Soil & the Supermarket-Pot Rescue

One plant, one pot. Those supermarket herb pots contain 10–20 seedlings crammed together — a marketing trick that looks lush and dies fast. On day one, gently tease the root ball apart and pot up the strongest 2–3 seedlings per 6-inch pot. It looks sparse for two weeks, then explodes.

Drainage is non-negotiable. Herbs rot in standing water faster than almost any houseplant. Every pot needs drainage holes and a saucer you actually empty. Decorative cache pots without holes are herb killers — use them only as outer sleeves.

Soil: Standard potting mix with extra perlite or coarse sand mixed in (about 1 part perlite to 3 parts mix). Herbs want soil that drains fast — dense, moisture-holding mixes stay soggy and invite root rot. Skip garden soil entirely; it compacts in pots and brings pests.

Pot size: 6 inches minimum per plant, 8–10 inches better for heavy feeders like basil and parsley. Bigger pots dry slower, which is a feature for forgetful waterers.

Watering: The Finger Test, Herb Edition

Our full watering guide covers the method; the herb-specific version:

  • Mediterranean herbs (thyme, oregano, rosemary): let the top inch dry out fully between waterings. They prefer a slight drought to wet feet.
  • Soft herbs (basil, parsley, cilantro, chives): keep soil lightly, evenly moist. Water when the top half-inch is dry — never let them wilt completely, but never leave them soggy.
  • Mint: the exception that likes moisture. Keep it evenly moist; it is the hardest herb to overwater.

Water thoroughly until it drains from the bottom, then empty the saucer. The "little sip every day" habit is how herbs die — shallow watering keeps roots at the surface and salts build up.

Winter adjustment: Growth slows in short days, so water less often. The number-one winter herb killer is the summer watering schedule applied to a barely-growing plant. Our winter-proofing guide covers the full seasonal shift.

Harvesting: The One-Third Rule (and Why Pinching Matters)

Most people harvest wrong: they pluck a few leaves from the top and wonder why the plant gets leggy and dies. The rules:

  1. Never take more than one-third of the plant's foliage at once. The leaves are the kitchen — remove too many and the plant starves.
  2. Pinch, don't pluck. For basil, mint, and oregano, pinch stems just above a leaf node (where two leaves meet the stem). Two new stems grow from that point — pinching makes the plant bushier, not barer.
  3. Harvest from the top down. Cutting the top encourages side growth; stripping lower leaves just leaves a bare stalk.
  4. Morning is best. Essential oils peak in the morning before the heat of the day — better flavor.
  5. Use it or lose it. Herbs harvested but unused are wasted growth the plant spent energy on. If you have a glut, chop and freeze in ice-cube trays with olive oil.

Flowers: When herbs flower (bolt), leaf flavor drops and the plant thinks its job is done. Pinch flower buds off the moment you see them — for basil this is a weekly summer chore. The exception: let chives flower once; the purple blooms are edible and gorgeous.

Feeding: Less Than You Think

Herbs are light feeders — too much fertilizer gives you big bland leaves with weak flavor. The rule:

  • In pots with fresh potting mix: no fertilizer for the first 2–3 months. The mix already contains nutrients.
  • After that: half-strength liquid fertilizer once a month during the growing season (spring–summer). Skip winter feeding entirely — the plant is barely growing.
  • The flavor secret: slightly under-fertilized herbs taste stronger. Stress concentrates essential oils — one reason Mediterranean herbs from poor soil taste better than pampered ones.

Troubleshooting: Leggy Stems, Yellow Leaves & Gnats

Leggy, stretched stems with sparse leaves. Not enough light — the plant is reaching. Move it to a brighter spot or add a grow light, then pinch back the leggy growth to encourage bushiness. Leggy growth never un-stretches; new growth will be compact.

Yellow lower leaves, soil constantly damp. Overwatering. Let the pot dry out, check that drainage holes are not blocked, and empty the saucer habitually. Our yellow-leaves guide walks through the same diagnosis.

Tiny black flies around the pots. Fungus gnats — they love the constantly-moist soil that soft herbs want. Let the top inch dry between waterings (herbs tolerate this better than gnats do), and use yellow sticky traps for the adults. A layer of sand on the soil surface breaks their breeding cycle.

White powdery coating on leaves. Powdery mildew — poor air circulation plus damp leaves. Space pots apart, water the soil not the leaves, and move the plant where air moves. Remove badly affected leaves.

Basil suddenly wilted despite wet soil. Could be fusarium wilt (a soil fungus) — unfortunately fatal and the reason to never reuse potting soil for basil. Start fresh with new mix.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you really grow herbs indoors year-round?

Yes — with the right herbs and enough light. Chives, thyme, mint, and oregano genuinely produce year-round on a bright windowsill. Basil and cilantro are seasonal even indoors; plan for them in the bright months and buy fresh in winter.

How much light do indoor herbs need?

Six or more hours of direct sun daily — more than most houseplants. A south or west window, or a full-spectrum LED grow light 6–8 inches above the plants for 12–14 hours a day.

Why does my supermarket basil always die?

It is 15 seedlings crammed in a tiny pot with no drainage, grown in a greenhouse, then shocked by your home's light and air. Divide it into bigger pots on day one, give it your brightest spot, and accept that winter basil is hard for everyone.

Can I grow herbs without a grow light?

Yes, if you have a genuinely sunny south or west-facing window. Without one, stick to the forgiving tier (chives, mint) and keep expectations modest — or get the light. It pays for itself in a month of not replacing dead plants.

How often should I fertilize indoor herbs?

Barely. No fertilizer for the first 2–3 months in fresh potting mix, then half-strength liquid feed once a month in spring and summer only. Under-fertilized herbs actually taste stronger.

Final Thoughts

An indoor herb garden is not about turning your kitchen into a farm — it is about four pots on a sunny sill that pay for themselves. Start with chives and thyme, give them real light, water with the finger test, and pinch instead of pluck. Accept that basil is a summer romance and winter is for the hardy herbs. Do that, and you will be the person snipping fresh thyme into dinner in January while everyone else is buying sad plastic packets — which is, honestly, the whole point.
