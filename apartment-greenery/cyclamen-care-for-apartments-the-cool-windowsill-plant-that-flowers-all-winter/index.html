Cyclamen Care for Apartments: The Cool-Windowsill Plant That Flowers All Winter

A florist's cyclamen is the plant most likely to arrive in an apartment in the cold months — a neat mound of heart-shaped, silver-patterned leaves carrying a crowd of swept-back flowers in pink, red, white, or purple. It is also the plant most likely to collapse within a fortnight, and the collapse is rarely the owner's fault in the way they think. Cyclamen does not want what most houseplants want. It wants the cold.

Here is the short version before the detail: a cyclamen in a flat needs the coolest bright spot you have, water given from below or slipped in at the edge so the centre of the plant stays dry, and an owner who understands that it grows through winter and rests through summer — the reverse of almost everything else on your windowsill. Get the cool spot and the dry crown right and a flowering plant can keep producing buds for weeks across the cool season. Park it in a warm room and soak the middle of it, and it will usually fade no matter how carefully you do everything else.

This guide covers the apartment version: choosing a healthy plant, finding a cool spot in a heated flat, the bottom-watering routine, why a cyclamen flops dramatically and what the flop usually means, feeding and deadheading, and the honest final chapter — the summer rest, what it involves, and why letting the plant go after flowering is also a perfectly reasonable choice.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • Cool is the whole point: cyclamen flowers longest in a cool room — roughly 10–18°C (50–65°F) is the range most often recommended. A bright windowsill away from radiators is usually the best seat in the flat.
  • Keep the crown dry: water from below, or at the soil edge. Water sitting in the centre of the plant is the usual route to a rotting tuber.
  • A flop usually means thirst, not death: a dry cyclamen can collapse theatrically and often perks up within hours of a proper drink. Check the soil before you panic.
  • Bright light, no baking: the brightest cool light you have. Leaves reaching and stretching means it wants more.
  • Twist off the dead: spent flowers and yellowing leaves come away cleanly with a twist at the base — no stubs left behind to rot.
  • It rests in summer: when the weather warms, the plant naturally dies back to its tuber. That dormancy is normal, not a failure — and keeping it for a second year is a project, not a promise.

First, Know What You Bought

The plant sold in supermarkets and florists through autumn and winter is florist's cyclamen, Cyclamen persicum, grown from a flattened, disc-like tuber that sits at the surface of the soil. Everything about its calendar follows from where its wild relatives grow: around the Mediterranean, where winters are cool and damp and summers are hot and dry. So the plant grows and flowers in the cool season and sleeps through the warm one.

That single fact explains most cyclamen trouble in apartments. The typical flat in winter — heated to a comfortable 21°C (70°F) or more, with dry air from radiators — feels to a cyclamen like the middle of summer in a drought. It responds the way it is programmed to: flowers finish early, leaves yellow, and it tries to shut down and rest. The care below is mostly about convincing the plant that it is still winter.

One more thing worth knowing at the start: the tuber is the plant's savings account. Leaves and flowers are replaced from it season after season, but if the tuber itself rots, the plant is finished. Almost every rule in this guide — the dry crown, the careful watering, the rest — exists to protect that tuber.

Light and Temperature: The Cool Spot Is the Key Piece of Kit

If cyclamen could ask for one thing, it would be a cooler room. Temperature matters more for this plant than for almost any other common houseplant, and it matters more than feeding, misting, or any other intervention.

What it likes

  • Bright light: a windowsill with plenty of light keeps growth compact and buds coming. Gentle direct sun through glass in the cool months is usually well tolerated; the plant is built for bright winter days.
  • Cool air: roughly 10–18°C (50–65°F) is the comfort zone most often quoted. Cool nights help flowers last longer.
  • A gap from the heat: keep the pot away from radiators, fan heaters, and the warm blast above a television or other electronics. Even a metre or two of distance makes a real difference.

Finding the cool spot in a heated flat

Most apartments have one, once you go looking:

  • The windowsill of the least-heated room — a spare bedroom, hallway, or a kitchen window often runs several degrees cooler than the living room, especially overnight.
  • Right against the glass on a bright sill is usually cooler than the middle of the room, though in very cold weather check that leaves are not touching frozen glass.
  • An unheated porch, enclosed balcony, or stairwell window can be ideal in milder climates, as long as frost cannot reach the pot.

Our guide to winter-proofing your indoor plants against the heating season covers the wider problem of radiators and dry winter air; cyclamen is simply the plant that feels it first and complains loudest. The warning signs of a spot that is too warm are consistent: flowers that open and fade within days, leaves yellowing from the outside in, and flower stems that flop and stay flopped even though the soil is moist.

Watering: Keep the Crown Dry

More cyclamen are lost to kind watering than to neglect. The tuber sits like a shallow saucer at the soil surface, with every leaf and flower stem rising out of its centre — the crown. Water poured into that centre sits against the tuber and the stem bases, and in a warm room that is an open invitation to rot.

The bottom-watering routine

  1. Check before you water, the same way we teach in our guide to how often to water indoor plants: cyclamen in growth likes its soil evenly moist but never sodden. When the surface feels dry to the touch and the pot feels lighter than it did after the last drink, it is time.
  2. Stand the pot in a sink, bowl, or deep saucer of room-temperature water for roughly 15–30 minutes, so the mix drinks from below.
  3. Lift it out and let it drain completely. Never leave the pot standing in water afterwards — the goal is a drink, not a bath that never ends.
  4. If you must water from above, aim a thin stream at the soil edge, under the leaves, and keep the crown itself dry.

It is the same bottom-up habit that suits African violets, whose leaves also dislike being wet — our African violet care guide walks through the technique in more detail if you want it. Consistency matters more than the exact method: moist, not wet; regular, not random.

The flop that usually just means thirst

The classic cyclamen emergency looks like this: you come home and the whole plant has collapsed — flowers face-down, leaves flat against the pot rim. Before assuming the worst, feel the soil. A dry cyclamen wilts dramatically as a defence, and if thirst is the cause, it often recovers within a few hours of a thorough bottom-watering, stems lifting as if nothing happened. Results vary with how long it sat dry, but the recovery is common enough that watering is always the first move.

The flop that does not recover is different, and worth learning to tell apart: stems that feel soft or mushy at the base, leaves yellowing while the soil is still wet, or a sour smell from the pot point to rot in the tuber rather than thirst. That is the pattern covered in our root rot guide — and with a cyclamen, the honest truth is that a tuber that has gone soft is rarely saved. Prevention, in the form of a dry crown and a drained pot, is the real cure.

Humidity and Air: Comfortable, Not Tropical

Heated winter rooms are dry rooms, and cyclamen — a plant of cool, damp winters — notices. Leaves with browning, crispy edges and flowers that finish quickly are the usual complaints. You do not need to turn the flat into a greenhouse; modest, steady humidity around the plant is enough for most plants in most homes:

  • Group plants together on the sill so they share the moisture their leaves give off.
  • Stand the pot on a tray of pebbles with water just below the top of the stones: the pot stays dry-footed while the air immediately around it stays a little moister.
  • Do not mist the plant itself. Water beading in the crown and sitting on the hairy leaves works against the dry-crown rule that keeps the tuber safe.

Our guide to increasing humidity for houseplants in winter compares these options properly, including what is and is not worth buying. For cyclamen, the pebble tray and the cool spot together do most of the work.

Feeding and Tidying: Small Jobs, Done Cleanly

A cyclamen in full growth and flower is working hard, and a gentle feed supports it — but this is a light-touch area, not a rescue lever:

  • Feed lightly while it is growing and flowering: a balanced houseplant feed at about half the strength the label suggests, every couple of weeks or so, is a practical starting point. Stop feeding as growth slows towards the rest.
  • Remove spent flowers by the whole stem: follow the flower stem down to the base and twist it sharply so it comes away cleanly. Snipping a stem halfway leaves a stub that dies back and can rot against the crown — and a spent flower left to set seed spends the tuber's energy on a seed pod instead of new buds.
  • Do the same with yellowing leaves: twist off at the base, cleanly, as they fade. A tidy plant is also easier to inspect, and problems like rot show up earlier.

Keeping the Flowers Coming

A healthy cyclamen flowers in succession — new buds keep rising from the centre as older flowers finish — so the display can run for weeks across the cool season rather than in one short burst. The conditions that stretch it are the ones already covered, in order of impact:

  1. Coolth first. Every degree of unnecessary warmth shortens the show. A plant moved to a cooler sill commonly perks up and holds its flowers noticeably longer.
  2. Steady moisture. Repeated cycles of drought-flop and rescue are survivable, but they cost buds; a steadier rhythm keeps new buds forming.
  3. Clean deadheading. Removing finished flowers before they set seed keeps the tuber's budget pointed at flowers.
  4. Bright light. Buds that open small, pale, or not at all usually point back to light or warmth, in that order.

After Flowering: The Summer Rest

When days lengthen and rooms warm up, a florist's cyclamen winds down: flowering stops, leaves yellow one by one, and eventually the plant dies back completely to the tuber. Indoors this often happens in spring. It looks like death and is usually just the calendar — the plant is entering the dry summer rest it is programmed for.

You have two honest options:

  • Treat it as a seasonal plant. Enjoy the flowers for the cool months, then compost the plant without guilt. This is what many growers — including some who adore cyclamen — choose, because a second-year plant takes months of shelf space for an uncertain encore. Florist cyclamen is inexpensive and bred for one strong season.
  • Rest the tuber and try for a second year. It is a genuinely satisfying project when it works: flowers in the second season are often fewer than the first, and results vary with the tuber, the rest it got, and your autumn conditions. If you enjoy the experiment, here is the method.

How to rest the tuber

  1. As leaves yellow, reduce watering gradually. Stop feeding.
  2. When the foliage has died back, stop watering altogether. Twist away the dead leaves so the tuber sits clean.
  3. Store the pot somewhere cool, dry, and dark or shaded — a cupboard, a cool spare room, the back of a shelf — and leave it alone for roughly two to three months. A forgotten corner is fine; a baking-hot one is not.
  4. Check occasionally that the tuber is still firm and dry. A stored tuber that has gone soft has rotted, and there is no waking it.

Waking it up in autumn

  1. Bring the pot back into light as the weather cools. Check the tuber: it should be firm. If it has outgrown or exhausted its mix, repot now into fresh, free-draining houseplant compost, setting the tuber so its top sits at or just above the soil surface — never bury the crown.
  2. Water once, sparingly, then wait for signs of life: new leaf growth from the centre of the tuber.
  3. As leaves appear, resume the normal rhythm — cool bright spot, bottom watering, light feeding — and buds usually follow the leaves through the cool season.

Common Mistakes

  • Keeping it in the warmest room. The living-room spot beside the sofa is comfortable for you and summer-hot for a cyclamen. It is the single most common reason the flowers give up early.
  • Watering into the crown. A soaked centre is the classic route to a soft, rotting tuber — the one injury the plant cannot grow back from.
  • Leaving it standing in its saucer. Bottom watering works because the pot is lifted out afterwards. A pot that lives in water keeps the tuber zone wet from below as surely as careless watering does from above.
  • Misting the leaves. It feels like kindness and achieves the opposite of the dry-crown rule, wetting exactly the surfaces that rot first.
  • Snipping stems halfway. Half a dead flower stem is a rotting stub waiting to happen. Twist the whole stem away at the base instead.
  • Throwing it out at the first yellow leaf in spring — or drowning a resting tuber. Yellowing in late season is the plant clocking off, not failing. And a resting tuber needs drought, not drinks: watering a dormant pot "to keep it alive" is how stored tubers rot.
  • Burying the tuber at repotting. The crown belongs at the surface, where it can stay dry. A buried crown stays wet.

A Note on Pets

Cyclamen is a plant to place thoughtfully in a home with animals: the tuber in particular is considered toxic if eaten, so a cyclamen is not a chew toy and is best kept on a sill or shelf a determined pet cannot reach — or skipped altogether in favour of a safer plant. Quick Questions, Straight Answers

Why has my cyclamen flopped over?

Most often, thirst or heat. Feel the soil: if it is dry, stand the pot in water for 15–30 minutes and let it drain — a thirsty plant often lifts again within a few hours. If the soil is wet and the stems feel soft at the base instead, suspect tuber rot, which is a different and much less hopeful problem.

Where is the best place for a cyclamen in a flat?

The brightest cool spot you have: a windowsill in the least-heated room, away from radiators and heat vents. Roughly 10–18°C (50–65°F) is the comfort range most often recommended, and cool nights help the flowers last longer.

Can I keep my cyclamen for next year?

Often, yes — with the summer rest. Let it die back as the weather warms, stop watering, store the pot cool and dry for roughly two to three months, then repot in autumn with the crown at the surface and water sparingly until new leaves show. Second-season flowers are commonly fewer, and treating the plant as one-season-and-compost is an equally legitimate choice.

Should I water cyclamen from the top or the bottom?

From the bottom, ideally — or carefully at the soil edge. The point is to keep water out of the crown, the centre where all the stems meet the tuber, because a wet crown is the usual starting point for rot. Always empty the saucer once the pot has drained.

Why are the leaves turning yellow?

It depends on the calendar and the soil. Yellowing while the plant sits in wet soil in a warm room points to overwatering and heat — move it somewhere cooler and let the mix dry a little more between drinks. Yellowing as spring warms up, with the plant otherwise winding down, is usually the start of its natural summer rest.
