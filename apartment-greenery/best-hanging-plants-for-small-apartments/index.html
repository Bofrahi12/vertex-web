Best Hanging Plants for Small Apartments

Small apartments have a plant problem: floor space is precious and every surface is claimed. Hanging plants solve it with the dimension most apartments waste — vertical space. A trailing pothos in a bright corner or a spider plant cascading from a shelf adds greenery without a single square foot of floor. This guide covers the eight best hanging plants for apartments, how to hang them with a "no holes" lease, and how to water plants above your head.

Why Hanging Plants Win in Small Apartments

Three practical reasons. Zero footprint — a hanging plant occupies air, not floor space, which is gold in a studio. Better light — hanging near a window puts plants in the brightest part of the room. Bigger feel — trailing plants draw the eye up, making small rooms feel taller. Designers call it vertical interest; renters call it the cheapest makeover available.

One more advantage: elevation keeps mildly toxic trailers away from curious pets — the simplest pet-proofing there is.

Hanging Plants for Apartments: What to Look For

Not every plant belongs in a hanger. The best candidates share three traits:

  • A trailing or cascading habit. You want stems that spill gracefully over the pot edge — pothos, philodendron, string of pearls — not upright growers that will look awkward dangling.
  • Tolerance for your actual light. Most hanging spots are near windows (great) or in bright corners (fine for adaptable plants). Match the plant to the spot, not the other way around.
  • Manageable weight. A wet 10-inch pot gets heavy fast — keep ceiling-hung plants under about 10 pounds and always anchor into a stud, never just drywall.

The 8 Best Hanging Plants for Apartments

1. Pothos (Epipremnum aureum)

The undisputed king of hanging plants: trails for feet in any direction, tolerates low to bright indirect light, and forgives missed waterings for weeks. Water when the top two inches dry, roughly every 1–2 weeks. Snip leggy vines, root the cuttings in water, and tuck them back for a fuller plant.

2. Heartleaf Philodendron (Philodendron hederaceum)

Pothos's softer, heart-leafed cousin — equally forgiving, roots effortlessly in water. Same care as pothos, but keep it away from cold winter drafts, the one thing that bothers it.

3. Spider Plant (Chlorophytum comosum)

The classic 1970s hanging plant: arching striped leaves plus dangling baby "spiderettes" — the most dramatic cascade here, and non-toxic to pets. Bright indirect light; water weekly when the top inch dries. Brown tips usually mean fluoride or inconsistent watering; neither is fatal.

4. String of Pearls (Senecio rowleyanus)

Strands of green beads spilling over the pot — stunning, and the fussiest here. As a succulent it wants a sunny window and water only when pearls look slightly deflated, every 2–3 weeks. Overwatering kills it fast.

5. Boston Fern (Nephrolepis exaltata)

Big, feathery, unapologetically lush — but thirsty and humidity-hungry. Give it bright indirect light, consistently moist soil, and regular misting; a steamy bathroom window is fern paradise. Water when the top inch dries, often twice weekly in summer. Skip it if you travel often.

6. Hoya (Hoya carnosa)

Thick, waxy leaves on slow vines that eventually produce fragrant star-shaped flower clusters — given bright indirect light and benign neglect. Let it dry out between waterings (every 2–3 weeks); hoyas bloom better slightly root-bound, so do not rush to repot.

7. English Ivy (Hedera helix)

Fast and vigorous — several feet of trailing growth per season, the quickest way to fill an empty corner. Bright indirect light, evenly moist soil. Warnings: prone to spider mites in dry air (wipe leaves preventively), and toxic to pets, so hang high.

8. Burro's Tail (Sedum morganianum)

Plump blue-green strands trailing three feet or more — spectacular where nothing brushes it, since leaves knock off easily (save fallen ones; they propagate). Give it your brightest spot and water only when fully dry, every 3–4 weeks.

How to Hang Plants Without Drilling (Renter-Friendly)

"No holes in the walls" does not mean "no hanging plants." These methods need zero drilling:

  • Tension rods. A shower-style tension rod across a window frame holds several small hanging plants where light is best — check the weight rating.
  • Adhesive hooks. Heavy-duty adhesive hooks stick to smooth walls, tile, and glass and remove cleanly — best for lighter 6-inch pots.
  • S-hooks on existing fixtures. Curtain rods, closet rods, exposed pipes, and sturdy shelf brackets all accept S-hooks instantly. Look around — most apartments already have hangable spots.
  • Over-the-door hooks. The back of a door near a window is wasted vertical space. A sturdy over-door hook holds a surprising amount of weight.
  • Macrame hangers on a wall-mounted shelf. A small floating shelf plus a macrame hanger turns one plant into a styled vignette.
  • Clothing rack or garment stand. A freestanding rack near a window becomes a dedicated plant station — no wall contact at all.

Whatever you use, test the weight with freshly watered soil — its heaviest state — and give a gentle tug test first.

Watering Plants That Live Above Your Head

Hanging plants dry faster than floor plants (airflow all around), so check them a bit more often:

  • Take them down to water. The simplest method: unhook, water thoroughly in the sink, let drain, rehang. No drips on furniture, no guessing.
  • Use a long-spout watering can. For plants you cannot easily move, a narrow spout reaches the soil without showering the leaves — or you.
  • Put a saucer or drip tray inside the hanger. A plastic saucer tucked inside the decorative pot catches drainage. Empty it after watering.
  • Self-watering hanging pots exist. Wick-based hanging planters keep moisture-lovers like ferns evenly damp for a week or more — genuinely useful, not a gimmick, for the right plants.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the easiest hanging plant for a complete beginner?

Pothos, without contest. It tolerates low light, forgives forgotten waterings, tells you clearly when it is thirsty (leaves droop slightly), and propagates from cuttings in a glass of water. If you can keep one plant alive, make it a pothos.

How much light do hanging plants need?

Most trailing houseplants want bright indirect light — the kind you get a few feet from a window. Hanging near (not in) a bright window is ideal for pothos, philodendron, spider plants, and hoyas. Succulent trailers like string of pearls and burro's tail want the brightest spot available, ideally some direct sun.

Are hanging plants safe for cats and dogs?

It depends on the plant, not the height. Spider plants are non-toxic and the safest pick. Pothos, philodendron, English ivy, and string of pearls are all mildly to moderately toxic if chewed — hanging keeps them out of reach, but a determined cat can still bat at trailing vines. For homes with dedicated plant-chewers, stick with spider plants and true palms.

My trailing plant is leggy with bare vines. How do I fix it?

Legginess almost always means too little light. Move it closer to a window, prune bare vines back to a leaf node, root the tip cuttings in water, and tuck them back into the pot. More light plus fresh cuttings restores fullness in a couple of months.

Final Thoughts

The best hanging plants for apartments are the tough trailing workhorses that thrive on benign neglect: pothos, philodendron, spider plants, hoyas. Start with one near your brightest window, hang it without drilling, and watch it spill and climb. In a small apartment, the air above your furniture is free real estate. Plant it.
