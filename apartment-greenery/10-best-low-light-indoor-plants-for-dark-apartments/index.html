10 Best Low-Light Indoor Plants for Dark Apartments

You've got a north-facing window, a hallway that never sees the sun, or an apartment where the only "bright spot" is three feet from the TV. And every plant you've brought home has slowly faded into a leggy, pale ghost of itself.

Here's the good news: the problem usually isn't you. It's the plant. Most popular houseplants are sold as "easy" when what they really need is a bright window you don't have. But a small group of genuinely tough plants evolved for forest floors and shady understories — and they will happily live in the dim corners of your apartment for years.

Below are ten low-light indoor plants that actually tolerate dark apartments, with honest care notes for each: how much light they really need, how often to water, and the one mistake that kills them.

What "Low Light" Actually Means

Before the list, a quick reality check, because "low light" on a plant tag is one of the most misunderstood phrases in houseplant care.

Low light means: a spot a few feet from a north-facing window, or near an east/west window blocked by buildings or curtains. In plain terms, you can comfortably read a book there during the day without turning on a lamp, but it's noticeably dimmer than the windowsill. If you want numbers, that's roughly 50–250 foot-candles — bright shade, not a closet.

Low light does NOT mean: no light at all. No plant survives in a windowless bathroom or a dark hallway with zero daylight. If a room has no natural light whatsoever, even a snake plant will eventually decline. For those rooms, a small LED grow light (we cover those in a separate guide) is the honest answer.

One more thing: "tolerates low light" means the plant survives and stays healthy in dim conditions — not that it grows fast. Expect slower growth and slightly less vibrant color than the same plant in bright indirect light. That's normal, not a problem.

The 10 Best Low-Light Indoor Plants

1. Snake Plant (Sansevieria trifasciata)

The undisputed champion of dark apartments. Snake plants tolerate very dim corners, need water only every 3–4 weeks (less in winter), and shrug off the dry air of heated apartments. They grow upright, so they fit narrow spots beside furniture.

The one mistake: overwatering. In low light the soil stays wet much longer. If the leaves go soft and mushy at the base, that's rot from too much water, not too little. When in doubt, wait another week.

2. ZZ Plant (Zamioculcas zamiifolia)

The ZZ plant stores water in thick underground rhizomes, which makes it nearly indestructible. It handles dim offices and dark living rooms, and only needs water every 3–4 weeks — sometimes less. Its glossy, waxy leaves also resist dust better than most plants.

The one mistake: repotting too often or into too-big pots. ZZ plants like being slightly snug. An oversized pot holds excess wet soil around the rhizomes and invites rot.

3. Pothos (Epipremnum aureum)

Pothos is the classic trailing plant for a reason: it adapts to almost anything. In low light it grows more slowly and the variegated types (like Golden or Marble Queen) may revert toward solid green — that's the plant's way of maximizing photosynthesis, and it's harmless. Water when the top 2 inches of soil are dry, roughly every 1–2 weeks.

The one mistake: letting it stay bone-dry for weeks on end because "it's easy." Pothos forgives a missed watering, but chronic underwatering causes the lower leaves to yellow and drop.

4. Heartleaf Philodendron (Philodendron hederaceum)

Pothos's close cousin, with softer heart-shaped leaves and the same forgiving nature. It trails beautifully from shelves in dim rooms and roots easily in water if you want free plants. Same watering rhythm as pothos: top 2 inches dry, then water thoroughly.

The one mistake: confusing it with pothos care-wise is fine — but heartleaf philodendron is slightly more sensitive to cold drafts. Keep it away from drafty windows in winter.

5. Cast Iron Plant (Aspidiolata elatior)

It earned the name. The cast iron plant tolerates deep shade, irregular watering, temperature swings, and general neglect better than almost anything else on this list. Growth is slow — sometimes just a few leaves a year — but it stays deep green in spots where other plants give up.

The one mistake: placing it in direct sun "to help it." Harsh direct light scorches its broad leaves. This is one of the few plants that genuinely prefers the dim corner.

6. Chinese Evergreen (Aglaonema)

Aglaonema comes in dozens of varieties with silver, pink, and red patterning — rare color for a low-light plant. It likes its soil to dry slightly between waterings (every 1–2 weeks) and appreciates average room humidity. The colorful varieties hold their patterns better with a little more light, but the plain green types are true shade tolerators.

The one mistake: cold damage. Aglaonema dislikes temperatures below about 60°F (15°C). Keep it away from cold windows and air-conditioning blasts.

7. Peace Lily (Spathiphyllum)

One of the few low-light plants that flowers — its white blooms appear even in dim rooms. Peace lilies are also famously communicative: the leaves droop dramatically when thirsty and perk up within hours of watering, so beginners always know what to do. Water roughly weekly, when the top inch of soil dries.

The one mistake: using it as a "set and forget" plant. Peace lilies are thirstier than everything else on this list. If you travel often, this isn't your plant.

8. Parlor Palm (Chamaedorea elegans)

The classic Victorian-era houseplant, bred for dim drawing rooms long before grow lights existed. Parlor palms grow slowly, stay compact for years, and handle the dry air of apartments reasonably well. Water every 1–2 weeks; they dislike soggy soil.

The one mistake: letting spider mites get established. Parlor palms are mite magnets in dry indoor air. Wipe the fronds with a damp cloth every few weeks as prevention — it takes two minutes.

9. Dracaena (Dracaena fragrans 'Mass Cane' or D. marginata)

Dracaenas bring a tree-like, architectural presence to dark corners where smaller plants get lost. The mass cane (corn plant) tolerates lower light than most; the dragon tree (D. marginata) wants just a touch more. Water every 2–3 weeks, and use room-temperature water — dracaenas are sensitive to fluoride and cold water, which can brown the leaf tips.

The one mistake: overpotting a slow grower. Like the ZZ plant, dracaenas in oversized pots sit in wet soil and develop root problems.

10. Spider Plant (Chlorophytum comosum)

Spider plants prefer bright indirect light, but they're adaptable enough to survive in lower light — they'll just grow more slowly and produce fewer of the baby "spiderettes" they're famous for. They're also non-toxic to cats and dogs, which matters in small apartments. Water weekly when the top inch dries.

The one mistake: expecting the arching, baby-covered look of the garden-center plant in a dark corner. In low light, keep expectations modest: healthy green foliage is the win here.

Plants to Avoid in Dark Apartments

Honesty matters more than optimism, so here's what not to buy for a dark apartment, no matter how pretty it looks in the store: fiddle-leaf figs, succulents and cacti (they need strong light or they stretch and rot), crotons, bird of paradise, and most flowering plants. They won't just grow slowly in low light — they'll actively decline. Save them for a future brighter home.

5 Tricks to Squeeze More Light From a Dark Apartment

Even shade-tolerant plants do better with a little help:

  1. Clean your windows. Dust and grime can cut incoming light noticeably. It sounds trivial; the difference isn't.
  2. Use light-colored curtains — or none. Heavy dark drapes can halve the light reaching your plants.
  3. Add a mirror or white surface nearby. Bouncing existing daylight toward the plant is free and surprisingly effective.
  4. Rotate plants quarterly. The side facing the room gets almost no light; a quarter-turn evens things out.
  5. Dust the leaves. A layer of dust on broad leaves like the cast iron plant's measurably reduces photosynthesis. A damp cloth every month or two is enough.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my apartment is "low light" or "no light"?

Try the shadow test at midday: hold your hand about a foot above a white sheet of paper where the plant would sit. A soft, fuzzy shadow means low light — fine for the plants on this list. Barely any shadow at all means very low light; only snake plants, ZZ plants, and cast iron plants stand a chance, and even they grow best with a small grow light supplement. No shadow in a windowless room means no plant will survive without artificial light.

Will these plants grow in low light, or just survive?

Both, but slowly. Expect roughly half the growth speed you'd see in bright indirect light. Snake plants and ZZ plants may push only a few new leaves a year in dim spots. That's healthy and normal — not a sign something's wrong.

Do low-light plants need less water?

Yes — and this is where most people go wrong. Less light means slower water use, so soil stays wet longer. The golden rule for every plant on this list: check the soil with your finger before watering, and water less often than the tag suggests in dim conditions. Overwatering kills more low-light plants than darkness ever does.

Can I use regular lamps instead of grow lights?

Regular LED or fluorescent room lamps do provide some usable light, and a bright desk lamp a foot or two from the plant genuinely helps. But household bulbs aren't designed for plants — a dedicated full-spectrum LED grow light gives far more of the wavelengths plants use, for similar electricity cost. If you're serious about a dark corner, a small clip-on grow light is the better investment.

Final Thoughts

A dark apartment isn't a plant-free apartment — it's just a specific plant apartment. Start with one or two of the toughest plants on this list (snake plant and pothos are the classic beginner pair), learn their watering rhythm in your actual conditions, and add more once you're confident. The plants above have survived decades of dim living rooms and offices; they'll survive yours too.
