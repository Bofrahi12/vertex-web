How to Keep Houseplants Alive While You're on Vacation: Plans for 3, 7 and 14 Days Away

The flights are booked, the bag is half packed, and then you look at the plant shelf. A long weekend is easy to cover. Two weeks in summer, in a warm flat, with a thirsty fern in the mix — that takes a plan, not a hopeful extra watering the night before you leave.

This guide is organised the way the problem actually arrives: by how long you will be away. Work out your trip length, sort your plants into three simple groups, pick the method that matches, and — this is the step that saves collections — test it while you are still home. Results vary by light, temperature, and soil, and a method that works well in many apartments can still fail in a hot, south-facing one. A test run tells you which one you have.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • Away 3 days or less: water thoroughly before you leave, move plants out of direct sun, and stop there. Most established houseplants handle a long weekend without any gadget.
  • Away about a week: group plants together, add a wick or a watering globe to the thirstiest ones, and move everything to the coolest bright spot in the flat.
  • Away two weeks or more: combine methods — grouping, wicks or self-watering pots, and the bathtub method for the plants that drink most — or arrange a plant sitter. At this length, a person is still the most reliable option.
  • Never leave pots standing in deep water for the whole trip, and never seal a plant in a plastic bag in direct sun.
  • Test everything for at least two or three days before you travel. An untested system is a guess.

Step 1: Sort Your Plants Into Three Groups

Not every plant needs the same rescue plan. Ten minutes of sorting saves you setting up systems for plants that never needed them.

Group A: The ones that barely notice

Snake plants, ZZ plants, and most succulents store water in their leaves or roots and are built for gaps between waterings. For a trip of up to about two weeks, a normal watering before you leave is usually enough — and for these plants, an automatic watering gadget is more risk than help, because their real enemy is soil that stays wet. Our snake plant guide and ZZ plant guide cover how little they ask for.

Group B: The steady middle

Pothos, spider plants, monstera, philodendrons, and peace lilies sit in the middle: happy with a weekly rhythm in many homes, uncomfortable after a long dry stretch in a warm room. These are the plants the grouping and wick methods below are designed for. A peace lily will also tell you clearly when it has had enough of waiting — its droop is a signal, not yet damage, as our peace lily guide explains.

Group C: The thirsty and the small

Ferns, calatheas, fittonias, herbs, seedlings, and anything in a small pot dry out fastest. Small pots hold little soil, and little soil holds little water. These plants get first claim on the bathtub, the wicks, and — for longer trips — the plant sitter's attention.

Step 2: The Day-Before Routine (Every Trip Length)

Whatever method you add on top, these basics do most of the work:

  1. 1. Water thoroughly, then drain. Water each plant until it runs from the drainage holes, let it drain fully, and empty the saucer or cover pot. You want evenly moist soil at the start — not a pot sitting in a puddle for a fortnight.
  2. 2. Move plants out of direct sun. A sunny windowsill that a plant loves while you are home to water it becomes the fastest drying spot in the flat while you are away. Pull plants back from south- and west-facing glass, or group them in bright indirect light instead.
  3. 3. Group the plants together. Clustered plants share the moisture they release, and the air inside the group stays a little more humid than the room around it. One shelf or one corner is also far easier to set up with wicks and trays than plants scattered through every room.
  4. 4. Lower the heat where you can. A cooler room uses water more slowly. If your heating or air conditioning has a timer or a holiday setting, a gentler temperature helps the plants as well as the bill.
  5. 5. Remove dead leaves and spent flowers. Dying foliage keeps drawing on the plant and can rot against damp soil while nobody is watching. A quick tidy before you leave is enough.
  6. 6. Skip the fertiliser. A holiday is not the moment to push new growth. Feed again once you are home and the normal routine resumes.

The 3-Day Plan: Do Less Than You Think

For a long weekend, the day-before routine above is the whole plan for most collections. Established plants in medium or large pots, in ordinary room temperatures, will not dry out dangerously in three days — and the most common holiday mistake at this length is overcompensating: soaking a plant that hates wet soil, or leaving it standing in water "to be safe."

Two exceptions deserve a little more:

  • Small pots and Group C plants in a warm, bright flat: move them to the coolest bright spot and group them tightly; that alone often covers a long weekend.
  • A heatwave forecast: if your flat gets very hot while you are away, treat a 3-day trip like a 7-day one and use the methods below for the thirstiest plants.

The 7-Day Plan: Group, Wick, and Slow the Drying

A week is where simple systems earn their place.

The wick method (best for Group B and C plants in small to medium pots)

A wick draws water from a reservoir into the soil slowly, as the soil dries.

  1. 1. Fill a bottle, jar, or bowl with water and place it beside — slightly above — the plant if you can; a stack of books under the reservoir works.
  2. 2. Cut a length of absorbent cord, cotton string, or a strip of cotton fabric long enough to reach from the bottom of the reservoir to well into the pot.
  3. 3. Push one end a few centimetres into the soil, near the edge of the pot. Put the other end at the bottom of the reservoir.
  4. 4. Water the plant normally first. A wick tops up moist soil far better than it rescues bone-dry soil.
  5. 5. One reservoir can serve two or three nearby pots, with a wick running to each.

Watering globes and bottle spikes

Glass globes and bottle-top spikes release water into the soil slowly. They suit one thirsty plant in one pot, and they are simple to set up — but how fast they empty depends on the soil, the pot size, and how the globe seats, which is exactly why the test run matters. Fill them during your test days and note how long they actually last in your flat.

Self-watering pots

If you travel often, moving your thirstiest plants into self-watering pots — a pot with a reservoir below and a wick or false bottom between — turns every trip into the 3-day plan. Our guide to repotting a houseplant in an apartment covers moving a plant without the mess, and a holiday move is a good moment to do it well before you travel, not the night before.

The 14-Day Plan: Combine Methods — or Book a Human

Two weeks is the honest limit of most passive systems in a warm flat, so combine them:

  • Group everything in the coolest bright room, out of direct sun.
  • Wicks or globes on Group B plants; the bathtub method (below) for Group C.
  • Self-watering pots for anything you can move in advance.
  • And still consider a plant sitter. A neighbour, friend, or colleague who waters once in the middle of a two-week trip removes most of the risk. Make it easy for them: plants already grouped in one place, a watering can filled and waiting, and a short note that says which plants want water and which — the snake plant, the ZZ — want to be left alone. Overwatering by a kind helper is a real cause of post-holiday losses; the note matters.

The bathtub method (for the thirstiest plants)

  1. 1. Line the bath or a large sink with an old towel to protect both the tub and the pots.
  2. 2. Add a shallow layer of water — a few centimetres is enough.
  3. 3. Stand Group C plants in it, in their nursery pots with drainage holes, so the soil can draw moisture up from below.
  4. 4. Leave the bathroom door open and, if the room has a window, let the plants have its light; if it has no window, this method only suits a short trip unless you add light — see our bathroom guide (article 28, in drafts now — link it here once published).

Keep the water shallow. The aim is soil that stays evenly moist, not pots submerged for two weeks, which invites root problems and a cloud of fungus gnats on your return — our fungus gnat guide is best read as prevention, not as a welcome-home task.

The Test Run: The Step That Actually Saves Plants

Set your chosen system up at least three days before you travel — a week is better — and live with it while you are home.

  • Check the soil each day the way you normally would (our watering guide covers the finger test and simple schedules).
  • Note how fast reservoirs empty and whether any pot is staying soggy rather than moist.
  • Adjust: a wick that keeps soil wet needs a thinner cord or a smaller reservoir; a globe that empties in a day needs re-seating or replacing with a wick.
  • Take a phone photo of the final setup. If a plant sitter steps in mid-trip, the photo shows them exactly what "normal" looks like.

Apartment Examples

The studio flat, one week in summer

Twelve plants on one shelf unit. Group A plants get the day-before routine only. The pothos, spider plant, and monstera are grouped on the floor below the shelf, out of the direct sun that hits the windowsill at midday, with wicks running from two large bottles. The small fern goes into the bathroom sink on a towel with shallow water. Test run: five days, one wick re-seated. Result the owner is aiming for: moist — not wet — soil on return.

The rented one-bedroom, two weeks away

The tenant cannot drill, fix drippers to taps, or leave windows open. Everything moves into the living room, the coolest room, grouped on a waterproof mat to protect the landlord's floor. Self-watering pots for the calathea and herbs, wicks for the middle group, and a friend with a spare key waters once in the middle and tops up the reservoirs. A note on the mat, not a text message, lists the two plants to leave alone.

Common Mistakes

  • Watering "extra" the night before and nothing else. One heavy watering does not stretch to two weeks; it just starts the trip with wet soil that dries on the same schedule.
  • Sealing plants in plastic bags on a sunny sill. A bag in direct sun can overheat a plant quickly. If you use a bag or cover to hold humidity, it belongs in bright indirect light, loosely fitted, and tested first.
  • Leaving pots in deep water. Shallow and temporary is the method; deep and fortnight-long is how roots suffocate.
  • Moving a sun-loving plant into a dark corner for two weeks. Drying more slowly is good; sitting in darkness is a different problem. Bright indirect light is the compromise.
  • Setting up an untested gadget the morning you leave. Every failure mode — the fallen wick, the globe that empties in hours, the reservoir that tips — is cheap to discover while you are home and expensive to discover at the airport.

FAQ

How long can most houseplants go without water?

It depends on the plant, the pot size, the soil, and the room. Plants that store water, like snake plants and ZZ plants, cope with the longest gaps; small pots and thirsty plants like ferns dry out fastest. Rather than a single number, sort your plants into the three groups above and plan by the thirstiest plant you own.

Do watering globes actually work?

They often help for a single plant over several days, releasing water slowly into the soil — but how long one lasts varies a lot with soil and seating. They are a practical starting point for a week away, not a promise for two weeks. Test yours at home first and watch how many days it really covers.

Is the bathtub method safe for all plants?

No. It suits thirsty plants that like evenly moist soil, for a limited time, in shallow water. Plants that prefer to dry out between waterings — snake plants, ZZ plants, succulents — should not sit in water at all.

Should I ask a neighbour or buy a system?

For a week, a tested system is often enough. For two weeks or more, especially in summer, a person who visits once in the middle is the most reliable option — systems plus a sitter is better than either alone. If you use a sitter, group the plants, leave the can ready, and write down which plants to skip.
