Alocasia Care for Apartments: Big Dramatic Leaves Without the Meltdown

An alocasia usually arrives home looking like a piece of sculpture — big, arrow-shaped leaves with bold veins on thick stems. A few weeks later, in many flats, the oldest leaf flops over, another turns yellow, and the newest leaf's edges crisp up. It is not bad luck. Alocasias are tropical plants grown in warm, humid, evenly watered conditions, and a heated apartment offers almost the opposite: dry air, weaker light than it looks, and watering that swings between forgotten and flooded.

Here is the short version: give it bright indirect light near a window, keep the soil evenly moist but never soggy, keep it warm and away from cold draughts, and raise the humidity around it. Do those four things steadily and the plant usually settles and pushes out new leaves from the centre. Results vary by light, temperature, and how dry your heating makes the air.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • Bright indirect light: near an east window, or a short distance back from a south or west window with a sheer curtain. A dim corner produces small, pale leaves.
  • Water for even moisture: check the top of the soil often, and water thoroughly when the top couple of centimetres feel dry.
  • Warmth matters: alocasias sulk below about 15°C (59°F) and dislike cold draughts from doors and leaky windows.
  • Humidity is the winter battle: group plants, use a pebble tray, or run a small humidifier nearby. Misting alone lasts only a short while.
  • One old leaf dying is normal: alocasias often drop their oldest leaf when a new one arrives.
  • Keep it away from pets that chew: alocasia leaves and stems are toxic if eaten.

Where to Put It: Light First, Then Warmth

An alocasia wants bright indirect light: an east-facing window with gentle morning sun, or a spot a short distance back from a south or west window, ideally with a sheer curtain between the plant and strong direct sun. Direct midday sun through glass can scorch the leaves, while a corner that merely looks pleasant to you is usually too dim — growth slows and new leaves come out smaller.

Check what is near the spot. Cold draughts from a frequently opened door or a leaky window are worth avoiding; alocasias are among the first houseplants to sulk in a cold draught. Radiators and heat vents are the other winter problem: a plant can sit in good light and still crisp if warm dry air blows across it all day. Move the pot, not just the watering can. Turn the pot a quarter-turn every week or so so all sides get the light.

Watering, Step by Step: Moist, Never Soggy

More alocasias are lost to watering mistakes than to almost anything else. The plant wants its soil evenly moist — not wet for days, and not dry for days.

  1. Check with a finger, not a calendar. Press a fingertip into the surface. If the top couple of centimetres feel dry, it is time; if they still feel moist, wait. The finger test in our guide to how often to water indoor plants is the same habit, applied here to a plant that wants checking more often than most.
  2. Water thoroughly and slowly, pouring around the whole surface until a little runs from the drainage holes.
  3. Empty the saucer or cachepot afterwards. Roots left sitting in water are the road to the problems in our root rot guide.
  4. Use room-temperature water where you can, and water less often in winter, when growth slows and the plant drinks less.

If you have overwatered and the leaves are drooping with soft, yellowing stems, do not water again and do not feed the plant — let the soil dry, make sure the drainage holes are clear, and give the plant warmth and light while it recovers.

Humidity, Soil, and Feeding

An alocasia in dry heated air loses moisture from its big leaves faster than its roots can replace it, and the leaf edges answer with crisp brown margins. Grouping plants together genuinely helps a little, and a pebble tray — a shallow tray of pebbles and water with the pot sitting on the pebbles, above the water line — adds some moisture as it evaporates. A small humidifier in the same room is the option that changes the air itself. Our guide to increasing humidity for houseplants in winter covers how to do this without making the flat itself damp. Misting is the weakest tool on the list: it wets the leaves briefly and then the air is exactly as dry as before.

The soil should hold moisture without staying heavy and airless: ordinary houseplant compost with some added bark, perlite, or coir works well, and the pot must have drainage holes. Repot in spring when roots crowd the surface, moving up just one pot size. During the brighter months, a diluted balanced houseplant feed given occasionally is plenty; in autumn and winter, stop feeding altogether. Over-feeding is a common cause of brown leaf edges, because excess salts build up in soil that is watered often and flushed rarely — if the edges brown, flush the pot once with plenty of plain water and skip the next feeds.

Reading the Plant, and the Winter Slowdown

One oldest leaf drooping and yellowing while a new leaf opens is often normal — the plant is trading an old leaf for a new one. Several leaves drooping at once, with soft stems, usually means overwatering or a cold shock: check the soil and the temperature before watering again. Brown, crispy leaf edges point to dry air first, underwatering second, and excess fertiliser salts third. Small, pale new leaves mean not enough light; move the plant closer to the window in steps over a week. Fine webbing or tiny speckled damage under the leaves is possibly spider mites — the shower-and-wipe routine in our spider mite guide is the practical first response.

In autumn and winter, many alocasias slow down sharply: growth stops, a leaf or two drops, and the plant looks like it is giving up. In many cases it is simply resting. Water much less, stop feeding, keep the plant warm and in the brightest spot you have, and leave it alone. A plant that has lost all its leaves over winter is not necessarily dead — if the base and the corm underneath are still firm, keep the pot barely moist in a warm spot and wait for spring. When you repot, you will often find small round corms in the soil; each firm corm is a potential new plant. Set them in damp moss or light compost, keep them warm and humid, and pot them up once they have a leaf and roots of their own.

Common Mistakes

  • Watering by the calendar. An alocasia's thirst changes with the weather, the heating, and the size of the pot.
  • Letting the pot stand in water. The fastest route to root rot.
  • Putting it in a dark corner because it looks good there. A dramatic plant in a dim spot slowly starves.
  • Panicking at the first yellow leaf. One old leaf dropping as a new one opens is the plant's normal rhythm.
  • Feeding a struggling plant. Brown edges from excess salts get worse with more feed.
  • Throwing away a dormant plant in winter. Check the corm first — firm means alive.

Quick Questions, Straight Answers

Why is my alocasia drooping?

The usual causes, in order, are overwatering (soft stems, wet soil), a cold draught or a sudden chill, and underwatering (dry soil, leaves limp but stems still firm). Check the soil first — if it is wet, let it dry and check the drainage; if it is dry, water thoroughly and see whether the leaves lift within a day.

Why are the leaves turning yellow?

Most often the oldest leaf being traded for a new one, which is normal. If several leaves yellow at once, look at watering: soggy soil yellows leaves from the base, while a plant that has dried out repeatedly yellows from the edges. Cold draughts can yellow leaves too.

How much light does an alocasia need indoors?

Bright indirect light — near an east window, or a short distance back from a brighter window with a sheer curtain. Gentle morning sun is usually fine, but strong direct sun through glass can scorch the leaves. A dim corner produces small, pale leaves.

Does an alocasia need high humidity?

It prefers it — the big leaves lose moisture fast in dry heated air, and crispy edges are the usual complaint. Grouping plants, a pebble tray, or a small humidifier all help. Misting alone does not change the air in any lasting way.
