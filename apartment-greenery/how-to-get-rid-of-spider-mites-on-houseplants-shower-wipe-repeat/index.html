How to Get Rid of Spider Mites on Houseplants: Shower, Wipe, Repeat

The heating goes on, and a plant that looked fine in September starts to look tired in a very specific way. The leaves lose their shine and take on a dusty, speckled look — tiny pale pinpricks scattered across the surface, as if the colour has been scratched off in miniature. Turn a leaf over and there may be fine, silky webbing stretched between the veins, and specks so small you are not sure they moved. That pattern, in a heated flat, in autumn or winter, usually points to one pest: spider mites.

Spider mites are not insects and they are barely visible. They are tiny arachnids — relatives of spiders — that feed on the undersides of leaves by piercing individual plant cells and draining them. Each drained cell is one pale speck. A few dozen specks mean little; thousands of feeding mites turn a leaf yellow, then bronze, then dry and papery. The reason they explode in apartments in the heating season is simple: they thrive in warm, dry air, and a radiator-heated room is warm and dry for months at a time. The plant sitting closest to the radiator, in the driest air in the flat, is usually the first one hit.

The treatment is mostly mechanical, and it starts in your bathtub, not in a shop. Shower the plant thoroughly, wipe the undersides, repeat on a short cycle, and change the dry conditions that invited them. This guide covers confirming the diagnosis with a sheet of white paper, the full treatment step by step, how to protect the rest of a small collection, and the honest point at which a plant is too far gone. Results vary by how early you catch them and how consistently you repeat, but a light infestation found at the speckled stage often clears with water, wiping, and patience.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • The signature sign: fine pale speckling (stippling) across a leaf, a dusty or faded look, and — on the underside — tiny moving specks and, as numbers build, fine webbing between leaf veins and around leaf joints.
  • The white-paper test: hold white paper under a suspect leaf, tap the leaf sharply, then watch the specks that land. Specks that slowly move are mites; specks that sit still are dust or soil.
  • First move: isolate the plant away from the collection, ideally in a bathroom. Mites spread on touching leaves, on hands and sleeves, and on air currents from a radiator or fan.
  • The core treatment is a shower: rinse the whole plant in the bathtub or shower tray, aiming the water at the undersides of the leaves. This knocks off mites and webbing in a way no spray bottle misting can match.
  • Wipe what the shower misses: a damp soft cloth across the undersides of larger leaves removes mites, eggs, and webbing the water left behind.
  • Repeat on a short cycle, not once: eggs are not removed by one wash and are not reliably killed by most sprays. Re-shower and re-inspect every few days at first, then weekly, until several checks in a row come back clean.
  • Fix the invitation: move susceptible plants away from radiators and hot air vents, and raise the air moisture around them where you can — dry air is the condition mites are waiting for.

First, Confirm It Is Spider Mites (Not Dust, Mealybugs, or Thrips)

Spider mites are so small that most people diagnose them from the damage and the webbing, not from a clear look at the animal itself. Spend five minutes confirming before you treat, because the white and pale things on houseplants are not all the same pest.

  • Spider mites live mostly on leaf undersides. The top of the leaf shows fine, even speckling — many tiny pale or yellowish dots, sometimes a general bronzed or faded cast. The underside may hold reddish, brownish, or pale specks in fine webbing. Webbing is the late sign: a plant can carry mites for a while before any web is visible, so no webbing does not rule them out.
  • Dust is the everyday lookalike, especially on plants near a radiator or a busy road. Dust sits on the top of the leaf, wipes off evenly, and — the decisive test — does not move on white paper and does not produce speckled cell damage or underside webbing. A plant can of course have both: dust on top, mites underneath.
  • Mealybugs are much larger and unmistakable once you look: soft white insects, a few millimetres long, in cottony clusters at leaf joints. If your white problem is fluffy clusters you can pick off with a fingertip, that is a different treatment — see our mealybug guide instead.
  • Thrips are slender, slightly larger, and tend to leave silvery patches with tiny dark specks of droppings on the surface, rather than even stippling with underside webbing. The first steps overlap — isolate, shower, repeat — but heavy webbing points to mites, not thrips.
  • Fungus gnats are not this. Tiny black flies around the soil are a soil pest with a different fix entirely, covered in our fungus gnats guide. Spider mites live on the leaves, not in the mix.

Two supporting clues strengthen a mite diagnosis: the damage is worst on the side of the plant facing the heat or the driest air, and other soft-leaved plants nearby — ivy, palms, calatheas, crotons, and indoor herbs often show it early — start speckling in the same weeks. One dull plant might be light or watering; several plants speckling together beside a radiator is a pest pattern.

The white-paper test, step by step

  1. Take a sheet of plain white paper or a white plate.
  2. Hold it directly under a speckled leaf, ideally one with webbing.
  3. Tap or flick the leaf firmly several times so anything loose falls onto the paper.
  4. Wait half a minute and look closely in good light. Mites appear as pinhead-sized specks — reddish, amber, or dark — that begin to crawl slowly across the paper.
  5. If the specks never move, repeat the test on two or three more leaves before deciding. A light infestation sheds few mites per tap.

A phone camera zoomed in, or any small magnifier if you own one, makes this much easier — but the paper test on its own is a practical starting point used by many growers, and it is enough to act on when it matches speckling and webbing.

Why the Heating Season Is Mite Season in a Flat

Outdoors, spider mites are a hot, dry summer problem. Indoors, the calendar flips: the worst months are usually the months the heating runs. Three apartment conditions come together at once.

  • Radiator air is very dry. Heated indoor air holds little moisture, and the spot directly above or beside a radiator is the driest microclimate in the home. Mites reproduce faster in dry warmth and struggle in humid air — which is also why bathrooms and steamy kitchens are often, though not always, less affected.
  • Plants are crowded together for winter. Windowsill space shrinks with the light, plants get pushed shoulder to shoulder on the brightest sill, and touching leaves are bridges a mite can simply walk across.
  • Nobody is showering the plants. In summer a balcony plant gets rained on and hosed down. In winter an indoor plant may go months without water ever touching its leaves — exactly the undisturbed underside habitat mites need.

This is why prevention below leans so heavily on placement and humidity rather than on products: the same thinking behind raising winter humidity safely and winter-proofing plants against the heating season is what makes a flat less welcoming to mites in the first place.

The Plants Most Often Hit First

Any houseplant can host spider mites, but in practice the flat's early-warning list is fairly consistent: thin- and soft-leaved plants, and plants already stressed by dry air. Calatheas and other prayer plants (which are already humidity-sensitive — see our calathea care guide), palms, ivy, crotons, ferns, miniature roses, and kitchen herbs such as basil and mint are frequent first cases. Thick, waxy-leaved plants — a ZZ plant, a snake plant, a rubber plant — are hit less often and usually later, but "less often" is not "never," especially for a plant pressed against an infested neighbour. When you find mites, check the soft-leaved plants first, whatever plant you found them on.

The Treatment: Step by Step in the Bathtub

You need: access to a shower or a sink sprayer, a soft cloth, a spray bottle, and optionally a ready-to-use insecticidal soap or neem spray labelled for houseplants. Work in the bathtub or shower tray — in a flat this is the washable treatment station, and mites washed off go down your drain instead of onto the next plant.

Step 1: Isolate before you carry it anywhere

Move the plant away from the collection first, and think about the route: carrying a webbed plant brushing past the whole shelf can drop mites on everything it touches. Carry it low and clear of other foliage, straight to the bathroom, and keep it quarantined there or in a separate room for the whole treatment period. Wash your hands and change or shake out a sleeve that brushed the webbing before handling clean plants — mites hitchhike.

Step 2: Prune the worst leaves before you wash

A leaf that is fully bronzed, dry, or draped in webbing is holding a large share of the colony and will not recover its looks — damaged cells do not turn green again. Cut the worst leaves off at the base with clean scissors, bag them, and put them in the household bin. Do not shake them out over other plants, and do not add them to any indoor compost. Pruning first means the shower that follows is treating a plant you can actually clean, leaf by leaf.

Step 3: Shower the plant — undersides first

This is the step that does most of the work. Put the pot in the empty bathtub or shower tray and rinse the whole plant with lukewarm water at modest pressure, turning it slowly and aiming the spray up into the undersides of the leaves, along the stems, and through any remaining webbing. Mites cling, but a direct rinse dislodges a large share of the mobile stages and tears the webbing that shelters the colony. For a small plant, a sink sprayer or even a thorough rinse under a tap works; for a fragile or very fine-leaved plant, use gentler pressure and more time rather than a blast. Let the plant drain completely afterwards and empty the saucer — a treated plant left standing in water trades a pest problem for the problem described in our root rot guide.

Step 4: Wipe the undersides the water missed

While the plant is still in the tub, support each larger leaf with one hand and wipe its underside with a damp soft cloth, working from the stem out to the tip. This is slow, unglamorous, and effective: it physically removes mites and the tiny pale eggs tucked along the veins that a rinse can leave behind. Rinse the cloth often so you are removing mites, not redistributing them. Small-leaved plants that cannot be wiped leaf by leaf rely on the shower and the spray step instead.

Step 5: Use a spray only as the follow-up, and cover the undersides

For a light case caught at the first speckling, showering plus wiping, repeated as below, is often enough. For webbing in several places or mites on several plants, a thorough spray of insecticidal soap (or a neem product labelled for houseplants) after the shower reaches the crevices water misses. Whichever product you choose: follow its label directions, spray out of direct sun, test it on one leaf first if the plant is delicate or fuzzy-leaved, and — the part most people get wrong — aim it at the undersides until they are wet. A spray that only mists the tops of the leaves barely touches a pest that lives underneath. Plain water in a spray bottle is not a substitute here; a fine mist wets almost nothing off. The shower is the water treatment; the spray bottle is for product.

Step 6: Repeat on a short cycle, not on a hunch

One session, however thorough, is not the whole job. Eggs survive washing and most sprays, and in warm dry rooms they hatch quickly, restarting the colony from the undersides you cleaned last week. A practical rhythm that works well in many apartments:

  • The first two weeks: repeat the shower-and-inspect cycle every few days — roughly twice a week — checking with the white-paper test each time.
  • After that: weekly showers and inspections while any specks still move on the paper.
  • All-clear: only after several consecutive checks with no moving specks, no new speckling on fresh growth, and no new webbing does the plant leave quarantine and go back to the shelf.

Judge progress by new growth. A plant pushing out clean, unspeckled new leaves while the paper test comes back empty is recovering. Old speckled leaves will stay speckled; they are the record of the infestation, not a sign it is still active.

Step 7: Treat the neighbours and clean the spot

Every plant that shared the shelf, sill, or touching contact gets the paper test today, soft-leaved ones first. Anything positive joins quarantine and the same shower cycle — in a crowded flat, treating one plant while its infested neighbour stays on the sill just schedules the next round. While the shelf is empty, wipe the sill or shelf surface itself and wash any saucers; mites wander, and a clean plant returned to a spot still holding strays from a webbed neighbour starts at a disadvantage. If several plants need treating, work from the cleanest-looking to the worst, washing hands between plants.

When to Let a Plant Go

Most mite cases caught at speckling are recoverable. The honest exceptions: a small, inexpensive plant that is webbed over almost every leaf, has dropped most of its foliage, and still sheds moving mites after several thorough cycles is a standing source of infestation for everything near it. Replacing it protects the rest of the collection, and that is a reasonable decision, not a failure — the same line we draw in the mealybug treatment. A treasured or mature plant is worth the longer campaign: prune it back hard, keep it isolated, and persist with the cycle while it regrows. What does not work is the middle path — a heavily infested plant left half-treated on the main shelf, reseeding its neighbours between sessions.

Keeping Mites Out: Prevention for a Heated Flat

  • Move the susceptible plants away from the heat. A calathea or palm spending winter directly above a radiator is in the driest air in the flat. Even a move of a metre or two, off the radiator's rising hot air, changes its odds — as does keeping plants out of the direct blast of fan heaters and vents.
  • Shower plants occasionally, not only when they are sick. A lukewarm shower every month or so through the heating season washes off dust, early mites, and eggs before a colony establishes — and because you are handling every leaf, it doubles as an inspection. Our watering guide's habit of checking the plant while you are already at the pot pairs naturally with this.
  • Raise the humidity around grouped plants where you can. Grouping plants together, pebble trays, and a small humidifier near (not on) the collection all nudge the local air in the direction mites dislike — with the renter's caution from our winter humidity guide: raise moisture around the plants, not dampness in the room.
  • Quarantine new plants, every time. Mites commonly arrive on a new purchase, including plants that look clean in the shop — a light load of mites is easy to miss. Two or three weeks apart, with a weekly paper test and underside check, keeps an introduction from becoming a collection problem.
  • Do the paper test at the first speckle. The difference between a two-shower fix and a two-month campaign is usually timing. Speckling plus a heated room deserves a test the same week, not a wait-and-see month.

Apartment Examples

The calathea over the radiator

A calathea spends winter on a sill directly above a radiator. Its edges crisp — familiar from dry air alone — but this time the leaves also fade into even speckling, and fine webbing appears between the leaf stems. The paper test confirms mites. Treatment: the plant showers twice a week in the tub, gets wiped leaf by leaf, and — the change that actually ends the cycle — it moves to a bright spot on the other side of the room, away from the radiator's dry updraft, near the rest of the grouped plants where the air is less parched. The speckled leaves stay speckled; the new growth comes in clean.

The kitchen herbs that seeded the shelf

Basil and mint on a warm kitchen windowsill speckle first, but they are small, cheap, and fast-growing, so they are ignored — until the palm beside them starts to fade. Mites walked from the herbs to the palm while the herbs were treated as too minor to matter. The fix here is order of operations: the herbs, too infested to be worth a long campaign, go in the bin; the palm gets the full shower, wipe, and repeat cycle in quarantine; and the sill is wiped down before anything returns to it. Small plants are not exempt from treatment decisions — they are where decisions are cheapest.

The ivy nobody turned over

A trailing ivy looks merely dusty for weeks, because all the evidence is on the undersides and inside the tangle where nobody looks. One leaf turned over during watering shows webbing and moving specks. Ivy cannot be wiped leaf by leaf, so its treatment leans on long, thorough showers — the whole plant swirled gently in a tub of lukewarm water and rinsed — repeated on the short cycle, plus pruning the worst tangles to open the plant up so water reaches inside. The lesson generalises: dust that only ever gets checked from above is how mite colonies buy themselves a month.

Common Mistakes

  • Misting with a spray bottle and calling it treatment. A fine mist barely wets the undersides and raises room humidity for minutes. Mites are removed by a real rinse under a shower or tap, not by fog.
  • Treating once. Eggs survive the first session. Without the repeat cycle, the colony rebuilds from the undersides you cleaned and the infestation "comes back" — it never left.
  • Spraying only the tops of the leaves. The pest lives underneath. Any product that does not wet the undersides is mostly decoration.
  • Ignoring the neighbours. In a flat, plants touch. An untreated infested neighbour on the same sill reseeds the plant you just cleaned.
  • Replacing the plant but not checking the spot. A new plant placed in the same dry radiator spot, next to the same unchecked neighbours, often meets the same mites — and stressed, shop-fresh plants are easy targets. If yellowing or speckling spreads to a replacement, our yellow leaves guide helps separate pest damage from watering and light causes.
  • Using a heavy pesticide indoors as the first move. In a small flat, the mechanical routine — isolate, prune, shower, wipe, repeat — handles most light and moderate cases without filling the room you live in with stronger chemicals. If you do use a product, follow its label exactly and ventilate the room.

FAQ

Can spider mites bite people or live on pets?

Spider mites feed on plants. They do not infest people or pets, and they do not live in bedding or furniture the way some household pests do — their habitat is the underside of a living leaf. Finding them on a plant is a plant problem, handled at the plant; no treatment of the room itself is needed beyond wiping the shelf the plant stood on.

Why do spider mites keep coming back after I shower the plant?

Usually because the cycle stopped too early — eggs left along the veins hatch after the last session — or because an untreated neighbour is reseeding the plant, or because the plant went straight back to the same hot, dry spot above a radiator. Extend the repeat cycle until several paper tests in a row are empty, test the neighbours, and change the spot.

Does raising humidity alone get rid of spider mites?

Moister air makes life harder for mites and slower for an infestation to build, and it is a genuine prevention tool — but on its own it rarely clears an established, webbing colony. Treat humidity as the condition change that stops the problem recurring, and the shower-and-wipe cycle as the treatment that clears the mites already there.

Should I throw away a plant with spider mites?

Not automatically. Caught at the speckling stage, most plants recover with the shower, wipe, and repeat routine, even if the damaged leaves stay marked. Replacement becomes the sensible option when a plant is small and inexpensive, webbed over most of its growth, and still shedding live mites after several honest cycles — keeping it then risks the rest of the collection.

Are spider mites the same as the tiny spiders making webs in corners?

No. House spiders are predators that happen to share the name; they do not feed on your plants, and their webs are in room corners, not stretched across leaf undersides. Spider mite webbing is much finer, sits on the plant itself — between veins, around leaf joints, over growing tips — and comes with speckled leaf damage. A real spider near your plants is, if anything, on your side.

Can I use the same treatment on every plant?

The mechanical steps — isolation, showering, wiping, repeating — suit most houseplants. Adjust the details: use gentler water pressure on fine or delicate foliage, be cautious showering plants with fuzzy leaves that dislike staying wet, and test any spray product on one leaf first.
