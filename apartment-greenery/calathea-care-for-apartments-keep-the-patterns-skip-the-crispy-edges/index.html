Calathea Care for Apartments: Keep the Patterns, Skip the Crispy Edges

People rarely buy a calathea by accident. You see those patterned leaves — stripes, brushstrokes, deep green tops with coloured undersides — and the plant comes home with you. Then, a few weeks later in a heated apartment, the edges start to crisp, the leaves curl in at the sides, and the plant that looked like artwork starts looking tired.

Here is the reassuring part: with calatheas, crispy edges are usually a routine problem, not a mystery disease. These plants ask for three things most apartments do not provide by default — steady moisture, softer water, and air that is not bone-dry. None of those requires special equipment. This guide sets up a simple routine, then works through the edge problems one cause at a time. Results vary with light, temperature, and your local tap water, but in many apartments the change shows first on the new leaves: they unfurl clean instead of frilled.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • Light: bright, indirect light — near a window with a sheer curtain, or a metre or two back from a sunny one. Direct sun fades and scorches the patterns.
  • Water: keep the soil evenly moist, never soggy and never fully dry. Water when the top couple of centimetres feel just dry.
  • Water quality: if edges crisp despite good watering, try rainwater, distilled water, or tap water left standing overnight before use.
  • Humidity: these plants are happiest with humid air around the leaves. Grouping plants, a pebble tray, or a nearby humidifier often helps — see our humidity guide (article 26, in drafts now — link it here once published).
  • Temperature: warm and steady, away from radiators, heaters, and cold window draughts.
  • Feeding: light feeding in spring and summer only, at a weaker strength than the bottle suggests — calatheas are sensitive to salts building up in the soil.

Meet the Plant: Why Calatheas Behave Differently

Calatheas (now often grouped by botanists under Goeppertia, though shops still label them Calathea) come from the shaded floors of tropical forests in Central and South America. That origin explains almost everything about their care: the light there is filtered through a canopy, the soil stays damp, and the air is humid year-round.

They are also relatives of prayer plants, and many move their leaves through the day — rising at night, lowering in the morning. That movement is healthy and normal. If you enjoy that behaviour, our prayer plant care guide covers their close cousins in depth; this article focuses on calatheas themselves and on the one complaint their owners raise most: damaged leaf edges.

One honest expectation to set at the start: a calathea is not a set-and-forget plant like a ZZ plant. It is better described as a routine plant — undemanding once its routine exists, quick to complain when the routine slips.

Light: Bright, But Never Baked

Patterned leaves need light to keep their colour, but the thin leaf tissue burns easily.

  • Good spots in a flat: an east-facing windowsill, a shelf beside (not in front of) a south or west window, or behind a sheer curtain.
  • Too dark: patterns fade, growth slows, and the soil stays wet too long — which invites root problems.
  • Too bright: washed-out patches and scorched edges that no amount of watering fixes.

A practical test: if the plant casts a soft, fuzzy shadow, the light is in the right neighbourhood. A hard, sharp shadow usually means the sun is hitting it too directly at that hour.

Watering: Evenly Moist Is the Whole Job

More calatheas are lost to inconsistent watering than to any single mistake. The soil swinging between bone-dry and soaked stresses the roots, and the leaf edges show it first.

  1. Check before you water. Push a finger a couple of centimetres into the soil. Slightly dry on top, still cool and faintly damp below = time to water. Our finger-test watering guide explains the method in full.
  2. Water thoroughly. Pour slowly until water runs from the drainage hole, wait a few minutes, then empty the saucer or outer pot. Calatheas like moisture around the roots, not a pot standing in a puddle.
  3. Keep the rhythm steady. In a warm flat this may mean watering roughly weekly; in a cool, dim one, less often. Judge by the soil, not the calendar.
  4. Use a pot with drainage. Without it, "evenly moist" quietly becomes "waterlogged at the bottom", and roots suffer out of sight.

The Water-Quality Fix Most Owners Miss

If your watering rhythm is steady and the humidity is reasonable, yet edges still crisp, suspect the water itself. Calatheas are known for reacting to the minerals and treatment chemicals in some tap water, and the salts from fertilizer make the effect stronger over time. Practical options, cheapest first:

  • Let tap water stand in an open jug overnight before watering — a simple habit that many owners find helps.
  • Collect rainwater if you have a balcony — plants from rainforest floors are built for it.
  • Use distilled or filtered water for a month as a test. If new leaves come out clean, you have found your cause.
  • Flush the soil occasionally: once in a while, water slowly and generously and let a good amount drain away, carrying built-up salts with it.

Damaged edges do not heal — judge every fix by the new leaves, not the old ones.

Humidity: The Apartment Problem (and Three Workable Answers)

Heated and air-conditioned flats are dry, and calathea leaves lose moisture faster than the roots can replace it — another road to crispy edges. You do not need to turn your home into a greenhouse; you need the air right around the plant to be a little kinder.

  • Group your plants. Leaves release moisture, and a cluster of plants shares a small pocket of more humid air. It also looks good — one shelf, several pots.
  • Use a pebble tray. A shallow tray of pebbles and water under the pot, with the pot sitting on the pebbles above the waterline, adds gentle local humidity as the water evaporates.
  • Run a small humidifier nearby during the driest months, especially in the same room as your most sensitive plants.
  • Choose a naturally humid room. A bright bathroom or a spot near (not above) the kitchen sink often suits a calathea better than a spot beside the television in a heated living room.

Soil, Pots, and Repotting

Calatheas like a mix that holds some moisture yet drains freely: a standard houseplant mix with some bark or perlite stirred through works well in many homes. Repot only when the plant is clearly crowded — roots circling the drainage hole, water running straight through — and go just one pot size up. Our apartment repotting guide shows a tidy, low-mess method that suits kitchen-table repotting.

Expect a newly repotted or newly bought calathea to sulk briefly. Keep its routine steady and give it a couple of weeks before concluding anything is wrong.

Troubleshooting Crispy Edges, Curling, and Fading

  • Brown, crispy edges and tips: the classic trio — low humidity, inconsistent watering, or water quality. Work through them in that order and judge by new growth.
  • Leaves curling inward: usually thirst or dry air. Check the soil first; if it is dry a few centimetres down, water thoroughly.
  • Whole plant drooping in the evening, fine in the morning: often just the normal daily leaf movement. Drooping that persists into the next day points to thirst or root trouble instead.
  • Fading patterns: too little light, or age. Move the plant a step brighter (still indirect) and watch the next leaves.
  • Yellowing lower leaves, one at a time: often an old leaf retiring. Several at once, with wet soil, suggests overwatering — ease off and check drainage.
  • Sticky leaves or fine webbing underneath: inspect closely for pests, especially in dry rooms, and wipe leaves clean while you decide on treatment. Dry air makes several common pests more likely, which is one more reason the humidity steps above matter.

Common Mistakes

  • Letting the soil dry out completely "to be safe". For a calathea, the dry-out is the damage. Evenly moist is the target.
  • Misting as the only humidity plan. A spray raises humidity for minutes, not hours. It is a pleasant extra, not a solution on its own.
  • Placing it on or above a radiator. Warm, dry, rising air is the fastest route to frilled edges in winter.
  • Feeding at full strength, all year. Salt build-up shows up as — you guessed it — brown edges. Feed lightly, in the growing season, and flush the soil now and then.
  • Judging a fix by old leaves. Crisped tissue never turns green again. Trim the worst edges with clean scissors if they bother you, and read your results on the new leaves.

A Note for Pet Owners

Calatheas are widely listed among houseplants considered non-toxic to cats and dogs, which is one reason they are popular in pet homes. FAQ

Why does my calathea close its leaves at night?

That daily movement is normal for calatheas and their prayer-plant relatives — leaves rise at night and lower during the day. It is a sign of a living, responsive plant, not a problem.

Can a calathea recover from crispy leaves?

The damaged edges will not repair themselves, but the plant absolutely can recover: fix the watering rhythm, humidity, or water quality, and new leaves should come out clean. Trim away the worst damage if you like; it will not harm the plant.

Is misting enough humidity for a calathea?

Usually not on its own. Misting wets the leaf briefly, but the surrounding air stays dry. Grouping plants, a pebble tray, a humidifier, or a naturally humid room all give a longer-lasting effect.

Which calathea is easiest for a beginner?

Owners often find the rattlesnake calathea (with long, wavy, spotted leaves) and the peacock calathea among the more forgiving types, but all of them want the same basic routine: steady moisture, indirect light, and kinder air. Pick the pattern you love and build the routine around it.

Should I cut off leaves with brown edges?

You can trim just the brown edge, following the leaf's shape, with clean scissors — many owners do this for looks. Leave the leaf on if most of it is still green; it is still feeding the plant.
