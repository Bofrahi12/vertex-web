Thanksgiving Cactus vs Christmas Cactus: How to Tell Them Apart (and Care for Each)

If your "Christmas cactus" flowers in November every year, nothing is wrong with it — there is a good chance it is not a Christmas cactus at all. The two plants are close cousins, sold side by side under each other's names all season, and the label on the pot is the least reliable evidence you have. The plant itself carries the answer, and it is written in the edges of its flat green segments.

Forget the flowers, forget the tag, and look at the segments. Pointed, claw-like teeth along the segment edges mean a Thanksgiving cactus. Smooth, scalloped edges with no points mean a true Christmas cactus. That one check takes ten seconds and settles the question more certainly than the bloom date, the shop, or the relative who gave you the plant.

This guide walks through the segment test, adds the backup clues (bloom timing, flower posture, pollen colour) with an honest warning about how far to trust them indoors, explains why shops sell one plant under the other's name, and then covers the care both plants share — and the few places where the timing genuinely differs in an apartment.

The 30-Second Cheat Sheet

  • The segment test decides it: Thanksgiving cactus segments have pointed, claw-like teeth along their edges. Christmas cactus segments are smooth and scalloped, with rounded edges and no points.
  • Bloom timing is a clue, not proof: Thanksgiving types naturally flower around November, true Christmas types around December — but growers force shop plants, and indoor light blurs the calendar, so timing can mislead.
  • Most "Christmas cacti" in shops are Thanksgiving cacti. Growers favour them, and the generic label stuck. Owning one is not a problem — the care is nearly identical.
  • Both are rainforest plants, not desert cacti: bright indirect light, evenly moist (never soggy) soil, and no baking on a hot sill.
  • Buds are set in autumn by long, uninterrupted nights and cooler temperatures. A warm, lamp-lit room all autumn is the usual reason for a bare December.
  • Once buds form, do not move the plant. Relocation, draughts, and sudden temperature swings are the classic causes of buds dropping unopened.
  • The third cousin: Easter cactus has even rounder segments with tiny bristle tufts at the joints, and flowers in spring instead.

The Segment Test: The One Check That Settles It

What most people call the "leaves" of these plants are flattened stem segments joined end to end. Botanists call them phylloclades; all you need is the outline of a single segment. Pick a mature segment in the middle of a stem — the tip segments are young and less clearly shaped — and run your eye (or a fingertip) along its side edges.

Thanksgiving cactus: pointed claws

A Thanksgiving cactus (Schlumbergera truncata) has two to four pointed projections along each side of the segment — little teeth that come to an actual point, like the tip of a claw. It is no accident that this plant is also sold as the "crab cactus": the segment edges look faintly like claws. If the edges could snag your attention with tiny horns, you have a Thanksgiving cactus, whatever the label said. The peach-flowered plant in this article's second photo is one — look at the green segments at the top right and you can see the pointed teeth clearly.

Christmas cactus: smooth scallops

A true Christmas cactus (Schlumbergera × buckleyi, an old hybrid) has segment margins that are rounded and gently scalloped, like the edge of a shell. There are shallow indentations, but they curve inward — nothing comes to a point. Run a finger along the edge and it feels smooth all the way. Genuine Christmas cacti are harder to find in shops than their Thanksgiving cousins, which is why the scalloped edge is the rarer sight on a windowsill.

The third one: Easter cactus

If the segments are rounder still — almost oval — with tiny tufts of fine bristles at the segment joints and tips, you are probably looking at an Easter cactus (Schlumbergera gaertneri). It flowers in late winter and spring instead. The rest of this guide covers the two autumn bloomers, but the care notes below suit the Easter type too, with its rest season shifted to late winter.

The Second Clue: Bloom Timing (Useful, Not Decisive)

The two plants keep different calendars. Thanksgiving cacti naturally come into flower in November, roughly a month ahead of true Christmas cacti, which aim for December. A plant that reliably blooms for Thanksgiving is telling you something real; one that waits until the edge of Christmas is offering a hint too.

Treat it as a hint, though, because three things blur it:

  • Shop plants are forced. Growers manipulate light and temperature to have plants in bud exactly when shoppers arrive, so a forced Thanksgiving cactus can flower in December — and arrives home with its internal calendar already bent.
  • Your rooms vote too. Buds are triggered by long nights and cooler temperatures. A plant in a warm room lit by lamps every evening may bloom late, early, or barely at all, whichever species it is.
  • Hybrids blur the lines. Many modern plants are bred from mixed parentage, and their timing — even their segment shapes — can sit between the two textbook descriptions. The segment edge is still the best single test; an in-between plant is best called a "holiday cactus" and cared for exactly the same way.

Two smaller flower clues can back up the segment test when the plant is in bloom. Thanksgiving cactus flowers tend to be held more upright and outward, while Christmas cactus flowers hang and dangle more noticeably. And some growers check the pollen-bearing anthers inside the flower: yellow anthers point to Thanksgiving, purplish-brown anthers to Christmas — the bloom in this article's third photo, with its clearly yellow anthers, fits the Thanksgiving pattern.

Why the Shop Label Is Probably Wrong

None of this is a conspiracy; it is mostly economics. Thanksgiving cactus is the easier plant for a grower: it propagates readily, grows vigorously, tolerates shipping in bud, and can be timed to flower across the whole holiday shopping season. The true Christmas cactus is a slower, older hybrid, less convenient to produce in volume. So the benches fill with S. truncata, the signs say "Christmas cactus" because that is the name shoppers recognise in December, and the mislabelling repeats every year.

The practical upshot is oddly comforting: if you bought a "Christmas cactus" in the last several years, you very likely own a Thanksgiving cactus — the tougher, more generous bloomer of the two. The only real cost of the wrong name is a month of confusion each autumn, when your plant flowers "early" and you wonder what you did wrong. You did nothing wrong. It is keeping its own calendar, and our full Christmas cactus care guide — written for these plants as a group — covers the October routine that sets up the bloom, whichever of the two yours turns out to be.

Care: What Is Shared, and What Actually Differs

Both plants descend from the same Brazilian rainforest habitat, where their wild relatives grow on trees and rocks in dappled shade. That origin explains most of their care — and why the differences between the two species are smaller than the difference between good and bad care of either.

Light: forest shade, not desert sun

Both plants want the brightest spot you have that is not direct midday sun through glass — an east-facing window, or a south or west window with the plant set a little back, works well in many apartments. Steady, bright, indirect light keeps segments plump and deep green. In dim corners both survive but grow pale, stretched segments and flower poorly. Direct harsh sun can scorch segments reddish-purple, which is the plant asking for shade, not more light.

Water: the cactus that is not a desert cactus

The word "cactus" causes more damage here than any pest. These are jungle plants with fine, shallow roots; they want a free-draining mix kept lightly moist, drying only slightly at the surface between waterings. Let one dry out hard for weeks and segments shrivel and buds abort; keep it wet and the roots sulk and rot — the decline pattern is the same one described in our root rot guide. The check-before-you-water habit from our guide to how often to water indoor plants applies exactly: fingertip in the mix first, calendar second. After flowering finishes, ease the watering back a little for a short rest, then resume normal care as new segments start growing in spring.

The autumn rest: how buds are actually set

This is the one piece of timing that genuinely matters. From early autumn, both species form buds in response to two signals arriving together: long nights uninterrupted by artificial light, and cooler temperatures than the summer room. A practical version for a flat:

  1. From early autumn (a few weeks earlier for a Thanksgiving cactus you want in November flower), move the plant to the coolest bright room you have — a spare room or an unheated corner beats the spot beside the radiator.
  2. Make sure its evenings are genuinely dark. A plant on a sill in a room lit until midnight every night is getting a shorter night than the calendar says. A room you do not use after dinner is ideal.
  3. Keep watering light and steady during this period — moist, never wet, never bone dry.
  4. Once pea-sized buds are visible at the segment tips, the delicate phase begins: return the plant to its display spot if you must, but then leave it there. This is the stage where moves cost buds.

How much darkness and coolness your plant strictly needs varies with the plant and the room, so treat the routine as stacking the odds rather than following a laboratory recipe. In heated winter flats the dry air is the other enemy; grouping plants together and keeping them off direct heat, as in our winter humidity guide, helps buds open instead of crisping.

Feeding and repotting, briefly

During the spring and summer growing season, a balanced houseplant fertiliser at the strength its label directs for regular use is a practical starting point for both species; stop feeding once buds form. Repot only every few years — these plants flower happily in snug pots — in spring, after flowering, one size up, in a mix that drains freely. Never repot a plant in bud: it reads the disturbance as a reason to drop them.

Common Mistakes

  • Moving a budded plant to a "better" spot. Buds form in response to the conditions in one place; a move changes light and temperature at once, and the plant commonly answers by dropping them. Admire it where it stands.
  • Treating it like a desert cactus. Full hot sun and a drought regime are for a different branch of the family. Scorched, purple, shrivelled segments are the usual result.
  • Overwatering a plant that sits still. In a cool autumn room the mix dries slowly. Watering on a summer rhythm keeps roots wet for weeks — check the mix, every time.
  • Keeping it in a warm, lamp-lit room all autumn. No long nights and no cool signal usually means no buds, however healthy the plant looks. Green and glossy is not the same as ready to flower.
  • Judging identity by the shop label or the gift-giver's memory. Segments first. A November bloom from a pointed-segment plant is a Thanksgiving cactus keeping perfect time.

FAQ

My "Christmas cactus" blooms in November every year. Is something wrong with it?

Almost certainly not. A plant that flowers reliably around Thanksgiving, with pointed teeth on its segment edges, is a Thanksgiving cactus doing exactly what its biology tells it to. Most plants sold as Christmas cacti are this species. Enjoy the earlier show — nothing in its care needs to change because of the name.

Can I make a Thanksgiving cactus flower at Christmas instead?

You can nudge the timing somewhat, but you cannot fully rewrite it. Delaying the start of its autumn rest — keeping it in a normally lit, warm room a few weeks longer before giving it long nights and cooler temperatures — tends to push bud set, and therefore flowering, later. Results vary a lot by plant and room, and a plant pushed too late may simply flower in January instead. Most owners find it easier to celebrate the November bloom and add a poinsettia or amaryllis for December colour.

Do Thanksgiving and Christmas cacti really need different care?

Day to day, no — light, water, soil, and feeding are effectively the same for both, which is why the shared routine in our Christmas cactus guide works for either. The difference is in the calendar: the Thanksgiving type runs roughly a month earlier in both its autumn rest and its bloom, so start its cool, dark-night routine earlier if you are timing flowers for a particular week. Identity matters for expectations far more than for the watering can.

My plant dropped all its buds before they opened. What happened?

Bud drop is usually a protest against change: the plant was moved, turned sharply, placed near a radiator or in a draught, let dry out hard, or kept too wet during the bud stage. Work out which of those changed in the days before the drop and keep conditions steady next autumn. The plant itself is rarely harmed — with normal care it should bud again the following season.
